Understanding particle interactions with matter is essential when constructing and operating particle detectors. This is because detectors are never sensitive to elementary particles themselves, but only to the signatures that the particles leave in the detector through interactions with the detector material \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. The topic of particle interactions is quite large, as different particles can undergo different interactions and the interaction types depend on the particle energy. Since in this thesis a muon detector is discussed, the topic is restricted to interactions of heavy charged particles (which includes the muon).
\paragraph{Energy loss in matter}
Charged particles lose energy when undergoing interactions in matter. A quantity commonly used in this context is the mean particle energy loss per path length in the material $-\left\langle dE/dx \right\rangle$ which is also called stopping power. Since the interactions also depend on the material density $\rho$, one usually weights the geometric path length $l$ with this quantity: $x=l\rho$. This gives the stopping power the unit $\si{\mega\electronvolt \; \centi\meter^2 \gram^{-1}}$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.

\subsection{Ionization and excitation}
\label{sec:Interactions-Ionization}
Charged particles can ionize and excite atoms of the medium, thereby losing energy. This effect is the dominant process of energy loss for charged particles, until radiative effects become relevant at high energies (sec.\ \ref{sec:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung}).
\paragraph{Stopping power calculation}
Generally, the stopping power can be described with an integral over all possible energy transfers $T$ for the collisions between the charged particle and the shell electrons of the material atoms \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}:
\begin{equation}
    -\left\langle \frac{dE}{dx} \right\rangle = n_e\int_{T_\text{min}}^{T_\text{max}} T \; \frac{d\sigma_a}{dT}(M,\beta,T)\;dT.
    \label{eq:Interactions-Integral}
\end{equation}
Here, $n_e$ is the target electron density and $T_\text{min}$ ($T_\text{max}$) the minimum (maximum) allowed energy transfer in a collision. The differential cross section $d\sigma_a/dT(M,\beta,T)$ is a measure of the probability of the collision process of a given energy transfer $T$ for a particle with mass $M$ and velocity $\beta=v/c$ ($c\approx \SI{2.998e8}{\meter/\second}$ is the speed of light \cite{PDG2024}). One needs to respect the fact that the minimum energy transfer can not be arbitrarily small in due to the quantized electron energy levels in the atom. When allowing approximations for heavy particles $M \gg m_e$ (which is the case for muons, cf.\ sec.\ \ref{sec:SM-Muon}), one can calculate the minimum and maximum energy transfer as \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}:
\stepeqlabel{eq:Interactions-Tmin}
\begin{align}
    T_\text{min}&= \frac{I^2}{2m_e c^2\beta^2},
    &
    T_\text{max}&= \frac{2 m_e c^2\beta^2\gamma^2}{1+2\gamma m_e/M+m_e^2/M^2},
\tag{\theequation, \theequationadd{1}}
\end{align}
\stepeqlabel{eq:Interactions-Tmax} \hspace{-0.7em}
where $I\approx 1.7 \cdot Z^{0.85} \;\si{\electronvolt}$ is the mean excitation energy of the atom with atomic number $Z$, $\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}$ is the Lorentz factor of the heavy particle and $m_e\approx\SI{511}{\kilo\electronvolt}$ the electron mass \cite{PDG2024}.
\paragraph{Bethe-Bloch formula}
Computing the integral from eq.\ \ref{eq:Interactions-Integral} with the Mott cross section, which takes into account the spin of both the electron and the heavy charged particle, yields the Bethe-Bloch formula for the stopping power \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}:
\begin{equation}
    -\left\langle \frac{dE}{dx} \right\rangle = K\frac{Z}{A}\frac{z^2}{\beta^2}\left[ \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2T_\text{max}}{I^2}-\beta^2-\frac{\delta(\beta\gamma)}{2}-\frac{C(\beta\gamma,\;I)}{Z} \right].
    \label{eq:Interactions-BetheBloch}
\end{equation}
Here, $A$ is the atomic mass number of the medium, $z$ the charge number of the interacting heavy particle and $K= N_Ae^4/(4\pi \epsilon_0 m_e c^2)$ a factor including the Avogadro constant $N_A\approx\SI{6.022e23}{\mole^{-1}}$ and the vacuum permittivity $\epsilon_0=\SI{8.854e-12}{\farad\meter^{-1}}$ \cite{PDG2024}. 
\newline The formula is able to describe the stopping power for heavy charged particles in the velocity range $0.1 \lesssim \beta\gamma \lesssim 1000$ \cite{PDG2024}. Generally, the stopping power curve behaves like $\propto 1/\beta^2$ for lower velocities and then reaches a minimum at around $\beta\gamma\approx 3.5$. Particles in this stopping power minimum are called \acp{MiP}. For higher velocities, the stopping power increases logarithmically $\propto\ln\gamma$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}. The described behavior can be seen in the stopping power plot presented above in fig.\ \ref{fig:Interactions-MuonInteractions}.
\paragraph{Correction terms}
Eq.\ \ref{eq:Interactions-BetheBloch} contains two additional correction terms $\delta$ and $C$, which are discussed next.
\newline When the velocity of the heavy charged particle is as low as the shell electron velocity, the approximate description of the energy loss with the mean excitation energy is no longer valid, since quantum mechanical interference effects start to become relevant. The stopping power deviates from the $\propto 1/\beta^2$ behavior and decreases again towards lower velocities. This so-called shell correction is described by the $C(\beta\gamma,\; I)/Z$ term and increases the validity range of eq.\ \ref{eq:Interactions-BetheBloch} to velocity ranges below $\beta\gamma < 0.1$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}.
\newline For high particle velocities, the stopping power also deviates from the $\propto\ln\gamma$ behavior due to polarization effects of the material and the fact that the electric field changes its shape for relativistic particle velocities. The density correction $\delta(\beta\gamma)$ accounts for this effect, leading to a flattened rise of the stopping power curve \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}.
\paragraph{Statistics of energy loss}
The Bethe-Bloch formula (with its corrections) describes only the mean value of the energy loss per unit length, statistically the energy loss follows a Landau distribution. Larger energy transfers can lead to high-energy electrons, which are referred to as $\delta$-electrons \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}.

\subsection{Bremsstrahlung and other radiative losses}
\label{sec:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung}
According to electrodynamics, accelerated charges emit photon radiation. In the case of charged particles in matter, the acceleration occurs in the Coulomb field of the material atom's nuclei. The radiated photons are referred to as Bremsstrahlung and lead to energy loss of the deflected particle.
\paragraph{Bremsstrahlung for electrons}
The energy loss due to Bremsstrahlung becomes dominating for particles with high velocities $\beta\gamma \gtrsim 1000$. For electrons, it can be described as follows \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}:
\begin{equation}
    -\left\langle \frac{dE}{dx} \right\rangle =-\frac{E}{X_0}.
    \label{eq:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung}
\end{equation}
Here, $X_0$ is the radiation length, i.e.\ the path after which an electron has only left $1/e$ of its initial energy due to the radiative energy loss. Calculation of the radiation length is extensive and usually includes approximations. From eq.\ \ref{eq:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung} it is clear that the particle energy decreases exponentially when radiating Bremsstrahlung photons \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}:
\begin{equation*}
    E(x)=E_0 \; e^{-x/X_0}.
\end{equation*}
\paragraph{Radiative losses for heavier particles}
Note that the description above is for electrons. Of course, heavy charged particles (such as muons) show radiative losses as well, however at even higher energies, since radiative losses are heavily dependent on the particle mass $dE/dx \propto 1/M^2$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. Apart from photon radiation, other effects such as $e^+e^-$ pair production taking place directly in the Coulomb field and photonuclear interactions have to be taken into account for energy loss calculations \cite{Interactions-MuonStoppingPower}.
\paragraph{Critical energy}
The energy at which the radiative losses exceed the ionization losses is called critical energy and depends on the particle type as well as the material. Due to the mass scaling of radiative losses, the critical energy of electrons $E_c=\mathcal{O}(\SI{10}{\mega\electronvolt})$ is significantly smaller than the critical energy of muons $E_{\mu c}=\mathcal{O}(\SI{100}{\giga\electronvolt})$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. The critical energy and the transition from ionization to radiative energy losses is depicted in the stopping power plot above (fig.\ \ref{fig:Interactions-MuonInteractions}).

\subsection{Coulomb scattering}
\label{sec:Interactions-CoulombScattering}
Charged particles can experience Rutherford scattering on the Coulomb field of the atom's nucleus. Scattering is also possible in the field of the shell electrons, however this contribution is neglectable for high particle  energies $E\gg m_e c^2$. The Rutherford cross section can describe a single scattering process at angle $\theta$ for a particle with momentum $p$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \propto z^2 Z^2 \frac{1}{p^2 \beta^2} \frac{1}{\sin^4\frac{\theta}{2}}.
\end{equation*}
\paragraph{Total scattering angle}
Several scattering processes occur when the particle passes through a material of thickness $x$. The total scattering angle distribution resulting from the consecutive scattering processes can be approximated with a Gaussian distribution $f(\theta)$ centered around $\theta=0$. The width $\theta_0$ of the distribution of scattering angles can be estimated with \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}
\begin{equation*}
    \theta_0 \approx \frac{\sqrt{2\pi} m_e c}{\sqrt{\alpha}} \frac{1}{p \beta} \sqrt{\frac{x}{X_0}}
\end{equation*}
where $X_0$ is the radiation length of the material (sec.\ \ref{sec:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung}) and $\alpha \approx 1/137$ is the electromagnetic fine structure constant \cite{PDG2024}.

\subsection{Other effects}
\label{sec:Interactions-OtherEffects}
Charged particles can experience additional processes when passing through matter, which however are of less importance in the scope of this thesis. For completeness, they should still be mentioned here.
\paragraph{Cherenkov radiation}
Charged particles passing through matter with a velocity exceeding the velocity of light in the medium emit so-called Cherenkov photons, which are typically in the optical range. The energy loss due to this process is neglectable, however some particle detectors exploit the process by detecting the emitted photons. The angle $\theta_c$ at which the photons are emitted in this process depends on the refraction index of the medium $n$ and the particle velocity $\beta$, allowing for velocity measurements of non-relativistic particles with this process \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}:
\begin{equation*}
    \cos\theta_c=\frac{1}{n\beta}.
\end{equation*}
\paragraph{Transition radiation}
Another effect is transition radiation, which implies the emission of photons when a charged particle crosses the boundary of different media. This process also does not contribute significantly to energy loss. However, the radiated energy depends on the particle's Lorentz factor $\gamma$, allowing to exploit this effect for velocity measurements of relativistic particles \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}.








\section{Gaseous detectors}
\label{sec:GaseousDetectors}
One of the two detectors used in this work is a gaseous detector, more specifically a drift chamber. Gaseous detectors rely on ionization, where the detector material is a gas. A passing particle loses energy in the gas while ionizing gas atoms, creating electron-ion-pairs. When applying an electric field to the gas volume, the created charges are separated and a current is measured \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Brief history of gaseous detectors}
Gaseous detectors have a long history in particle physics, marking the first step from scintillation detectors (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-Scintillators}) read out manually \enquote{by eye} towards detectors producing electronic signals \cite{GaseousDetectors-HilkeRiegler}. The first gaseous detector was described by Rutherford and Geiger in 1908 and was able to detect $\alpha$-particles from a radioactive source \cite{GaseousDetectors-RutherfordGeiger}. The Geiger-Müller tube introduced in 1928 offered much stronger gas amplification \cite{GaseousDetectors-GeigerMüller}. The introduction of \ac{MWPC} by Charpak in 1968 opened new application fields of gaseous detectors \cite{GaseousDetectors-Charpak} and paved the way for drift chambers and large-scale tracking detectors. While semiconductor detectors nowadays showcase better energy and spatial resolution and have therefore mostly replaced gaseous detectors for tracking purposes, gaseous detectors continue to be used in numerous applications. This is especially the case for applications which require large detector areas, where they captivate with reasonable prices \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. An example for this are the muon systems of both the \acs{CMS} \cite{CMS-Main} and \acs{ATLAS} \cite{ATLAS-Main} experiments at \acs{CERN} which consist solely of gaseous detectors. Also, research in the field of gaseous detectors is ongoing, which already led to novel high-performance micro-pattern gaseous detector technologies, such as the \ac{GEM} \cite{GaseousDetectors-HilkeRiegler}.

\subsection{Operation modes of gaseous detectors}
\label{sec:GaseousDetectors-OperationModes}

Depending on the voltage applied to the detector and the resulting field strength, the gas detector operates in different modes, which are described in this section. The different operation modes are illustrated in fig.\ \ref{fig:GaseousDetectors-OperationModes}.

    \label{fig:GaseousDetectors-OperationModes}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Recombination region}
When zero or a very low voltage is applied to the detector, the electron-ion-pairs created by the passing ionizing particle can recombine before they can be collected by the readout electrodes \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. This mode is therefore of no use for a particle detector. 
\paragraph{Ionization chamber}
For medium voltages, the primary electron-ion-pairs are collected by the electrodes. Since the energy loss of particles in gases is rather low, the number of collected charges per particle is also small.\footnote{In argon, a \ac{MiP} generates only $\mathcal{O}(100)$ electron-ion-pairs per $\si{\centi\meter}$, equivalent to created charges in the \si{\femto\coulomb} range which are (almost) impossible to detect without amplification \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.} Therefore, ionization chambers are rather suited for particle flux measurements than for single-particle detection \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Proportional region}
When the electric field strengths become larger with increasing voltages, the effect of gas amplification starts to play a role. The drifting electrons gain sufficient energy to ionize gas atoms themselves, leading to secondary electron-ion-pairs which can again trigger ionization processes. For a wide voltage range, the resulting charge collected at the electrode remains proportional to the initially deposited charge. The gain $G$ is defined as ration of final and initial number of charge carriers and reaches values of $G\approx 10^3-10^5$ in the proportional region \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Region of limited proportionality}
When the voltage is further increased, the proportionality between initial and final number of charges becomes invalid. This is due to space charge effects, i.e.\ clouds of slower moving ions which shield the electric field \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Geiger region}
In the Geiger region, the output signal becomes independent of the number of primary charges. Such a detector can be used for particle counting purposes, however the ability to estimate the particle's energy loss with the deposited charge due to ionization is lost. In the Geiger mode, the gas discharge is not confined towards the electrode, but extends over the whole gas volume. Such a detector needs to be quenched (i.e.\ the discharge needs to be stopped) with an external resistor in order to become sensitive again to detect the next particle. The amplification in this mode can reach values of $G\approx 10^8-10^9$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Discharge region}
When the voltage is increased even beyond the Geiger region, gas discharges occur where both electrodes become connected via a plasma channel. This discharge can be triggered by ionizing particles or spontaneously. With measures to suppress the complete gas discharge, this mode can also be used for a particle detector, e.g.\ in so-called streamer chambers \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.

\subsection{Drift chamber}
\label{sec:GaseousDetectors-DriftChamber}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Proportional mode
%    \item Constant electric field and drift velocity
%    \item Correlation of charge travelling time and initial ionization position
%    \item Drift chamber legacy in Aachen
%\end{itemize}
A drift chamber is a gaseous detector, which not only allows detection of ionizing particles, but enables estimations of the traversing particle's position within the gas volume. It is usually operated in proportional mode, however the amplification region where the gas amplification takes place is localized. There exists a significant volume where the created charges drift without any amplification. Typically, the gas amplification is created by utilizing an anode wire, where the strong electric field close to the wire is responsible for the amplification \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. The described setup and working principle is illustrated in fig.\ \ref{fig:GaseousDetectors-DriftChamber}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{GaseousDetectors-DriftChamber.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of the setup and working principle of a drift chamber. The electron drift distance $x_D$ is marked.}
    \label{fig:GaseousDetectors-DriftChamber}
\end{figure}
\newline Drift chambers can be seen as further development of the \ac{MWPC} detector type and are famously used in muon detection systems. These systems are usually located on the outer shell of a general-purpose particle detector, and therefore have a large sensitive area, while still requiring a good spatial resolution \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.

%\newline {\color{red} Add picture of drift chamber and drift cell}
\paragraph{Drift region}
The drift region is considered the sensitive region of the detector and usually comprises most of its volume. When a passing particle creates electron-ion-pairs in this region, the charges are separated through an applied electric field. In the drift region, the field strengths are not high enough to trigger gas amplification. Instead, the charges drift with a constant drift velocity $v_\text{drift}$, experiencing a force equilibrium between the electric force and a decelerating force due to scattering processes with other gas atoms. Note that this drift velocity depends on the electric field, the gas mixture and pressure as well as the particle type.\footnote{The drift velocity of electrons is significantly higher than the velocity of ions \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.} Typical electron drift velocities for drift chambers lie in the range $\mathcal{O}(\SI{50}{\micro\meter/\nano\second})$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\newline At some point the drifting charges reach the electrode and are collected. One should note that the drift with a known velocity $v_\text{drift}$ allows to estimate the drift distance $x_D$ of the charges and thus the initial position of the passing particle \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}:
\begin{equation*}
    x_D = \int_{t_\text{start}}^{t_\text{stop}}v_\text{drift}(t)\;dt.
\end{equation*}
This is possible with only a time measurement, which is however required to have good resolution $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\nano\second})$ if resolutions of $\mathcal{O}(\SI{100}{\micro\meter})$ should be reached. It is therefore possible to improve the spatial resolution of the detector significantly beyond the granularity of the readout channels.
\newline Ideally, the drift velocity is constant over the full width of the drift region, resulting in a linear space-time relation. This requires design efforts to make the electric field in the drift region as homogeneous as possible \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Gas amplification} Drift chambers usually have an anode wire for electron collection. Usually, the field strengths close to the wire are high enough to enable the amplification of the arriving electrons, thereby increasing the output signal amplitude. Usually the drift and amplification regions are not strictly separated, as in the described example of the anode wire (fig.\ \ref{fig:GaseousDetectors-DriftChamber}). This leads to non-linearities in the space-time relation, which create the need for calibrations \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.







\section{Scintillators and photon detection}
\label{sec:Scintillators}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Scintillator: Photon emission due to energy deposition of particle
%    \item Sensitive photon detection: PMT, SiPM (discussed in detail)
%\end{itemize}
The second detector type used in this thesis is a scintillation detector. Therefore, the working principles of scintillators as well as their readout should be briefly introduced in this section.

\subsection{Scintillators}
\label{sec:Scintillators-Scintillators}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Organic and anorganic scintillators
%    \item Plastic scintillator (that is used in this thesis)
%\end{itemize}
A scintillator is a material which emits (optical) photons when a passing particle deposits energy in it. The emitted light follows a characteristic spectrum caused by the material properties, one also speaks of luminescence \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. Scintillation detectors played an important role in the early days of particle physics, as they were used in the Rutherford scattering experiments of $\alpha$-particles with nuclei. While the scintillation light was initially observed with the bare eye, the invention of the \ac{PMT} allowed them to be used in modern experiments \cite{Book-Leo}. Recently, semiconductor-based photon detectors were introduced, e.g.\ the \ac{GAPD} and the \ac{SiPM}, which will be discussed in sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-SiPMs}. 
\paragraph{Scintillator properties}
Generally, a scintillator should exhibit a high efficiency for conversion of deposited energy into scintillation light, as well as a linearity between the number of scintillation photons and the deposited energy. Also, the time between energy absorption and photon emission should be short in order to have a fast detector. Apart from this, the optical properties of the scintillator should be such that the scintillator is transparent for its own scintillation light \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
% and the optical coupling to the photon readout does not cause large losses
\paragraph{Scintillator types}
One differentiates organic and inorganic scintillators by their material and scintillation mechanism. An inorganic scintillator consists of inorganic materials, which form a crystalline structure. The scintillation effect can be explained with the band structure of the electrons in the crystal. Energy deposition in the material can lead to excitation of an electron, which can then de-excite at a luminescence center in the crystal under photon emission. Usually, these luminescence centers are impurities added to the crystal via doping \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,Book-Leo}.
\newline Organic scintillators on the other hand consist of organic materials, i.e.\ carbon compounds, where the electronic structure of the molecules drives the fluorescent properties. Of high relevance are the delocalized $\pi$-orbital electrons of the carbon. Electrons become elevated to higher energy levels due to the energy deposition in the material and can de-excite radiatively, leading to scintillation light. A frequently used form of organic scintillators are so-called plastic scintillators, which embed the scintillation material in a solid plastic solvent \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,Book-Leo}.
\paragraph{Photon readout}
To achieve high photon detection efficiency, it is important to optimize the optical connection between the scintillator and the photon detector. A special emphasis should be put on material transitions in the optical path (ideally similar refractive indices) as well as the geometry to reduce photon loss. If the wavelength of the light emitted by the scintillator deviates from the ideal wavelength for the photon detector, one can also employ wavelength shifting materials which are able to convert photon wavelengths \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,Book-Leo}. There exist several devices for optical readout with (nearly) single-photon sensitivity. Among them are \acp{SiPM}, which are discussed in more detail in the following section (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-SiPMs}), as they are used in the detector presented in this work.

\subsection{Silicon photomultipliers}
\label{sec:Scintillators-SiPMs}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Geiger-Avalance Photodiode array
%    \item Sensitivity and performance
%    \item Output signal shape
%    \item Characteristic noise spectrum
%\end{itemize}
\acfp{SiPM} are semiconductor devices capable of detecting single photons in the optical spectrum. Even though their name hints a similarity to \acp{PMT} which were mostly used until recent times, they do not share the main characteristics: While \acp{PMT} rely on a photocathode for photon-to-electron conversion (exploiting the photoelectric effect) and a vacuum tube housing a high-voltage electron amplification assembly to amplify the signal of single photo-electrons, the \ac{SiPM} is purely a solid state device \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. The working principle of this device is briefly described in the following.
\paragraph{$p$-$n$-junction}
A diode consists of a junction of a $p$- and $n$-doped semiconductor. Doping means that foreign atoms are added into the semiconductor lattice, which increase the concentration of electrons ($n$-doping) or holes\footnote{Positive charges in the lattice are called holes and effectively describe a missing electron in a lattice atom.} ($p$-doping). At the $p$-$n$-junction, charge carriers drift towards the oppositely doped region because of the different densities of electrons and holes. Due to the electric field created by the charge displacement, which acts on the charge carriers, a force equilibrium is reached. The result is a so-called depletion zone at the junction without any charge carriers and an intrinsic electric field. Usually, one applies a reverse bias voltage to the junction, increasing the depletion zone width and electric field strength \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,Book-Leo}. Fig.\ \ref{fig:SiPM-pnJunction} presents an illustration of a $p$-$n$-junction with the described depletion zone.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{pn_junction.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of a $p$-$n$-junction in equillibrium. The two doping zones as well as the depletion zone is denoted \cite[modified]{pnJunction-Wikimedia}.}
    \label{fig:SiPM-pnJunction}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Geiger-Avalance photodiode}
Photons can excite electrons from the valence into the conduction band, which is referred to as internal photoeffect.\footnote{In contrast, the external photoeffect describes the ionization of an atom, i.e.\ freeing of an electron from the shell, due to photon absorption. This usually happens at higher photon energies.} The excitation results in an electron-hole pair in the semiconductor. In the depletion zone, the charges are separated and drift in opposite directions due to the electric field. The resulting current can be measured, making the device a photodiode which is capable of detecting optical photons \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\newline Modifications in the semiconductor doping profile as well as higher doping concentration enable amplification of the electron and hole currents (which were created by the arriving photon) in the depletion region. When applying high enough reverse bias voltages, the photodiode becomes a \acf{GAPD}. Its amplification $>\mathcal{O}(10^{5})$ is so strong that the depletion zone breaks down (i.e.\ becomes conductive) once one electron-hole-pair was created in the depletion region. This gives the \ac{GAPD} the capability of single-photon detection. A resistor is needed to quench the discharge in order to rearm the device. The name \enquote{Geiger} is chosen in analogy to the operation mode of gaseous detectors which exhibit similar behavior (sec.\ \ref{sec:GaseousDetectors-OperationModes}) \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{SiPM device}
A \acf{SiPM} consists of an array of $\mathcal{O}(10^{3})$ \acp{GAPD} and their respective quenching resistors integrated onto one semiconductor die. Fig.\ \ref{fig:SiPM-Structure} shows a photo of an \ac{SiPM} module mounted on a circuit board.

\newline Advantages of the diode array compared to a single larger-area diode are the reduced noise rate as well as the reduction of dead time effects, since not the full detector but only a limited number of cells is disabled during the dead time. Since the diodes operate in Geiger mode, the signal amplitude is approximately similar for each single hit cell. This means that the resulting signal from the \ac{SiPM} is discrete in amplitude. One refers to the signal amplitude of one cell as \acf{p.e.} \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,SiPM-Gundacker}.
\newline Two unwanted effects can occur during the operation of \acp{SiPM}: One is the potential crosstalk between cells, which is of probabilistic nature. Additionally, delayed afterpulses can occur, due to charge carriers being trapped during the amplification avalanche and only being released at a later time \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,SiPM-Gundacker,SiPM-Para}.
%\newline {\color{red} Add schematic drawing and photo of SiPM}
\paragraph{Thermal noise}
As a semiconductor detector, the \ac{SiPM} is subject to thermal noise. Thermally excited electrons in the sensitive region of a diode lead to the triggering of one cell, resulting in a noise hit. In principle, the noise hits of all cells are independent. When taking into account the probability for crosstalk, the noise spectrum of an \ac{SiPM} does exhibit noise hits with several \ac{p.e.} amplitude.\footnote{Of course, it is (in principle) also possible for noise hits of two cells in the \ac{SiPM} to happen independently within a short amount of time.} The frequency of multiple \ac{p.e.} noise hits compared to single cell noise hits allows estimation of the crosstalk probability of the device \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,SiPM-Gundacker}.
\newline Note that for each additional hit cell due to crosstalk, the crosstalk probability needs to be added as a factor to the expected rate. Therefore, one expects an exponentially falling behavior of the noise rate w.r.t.\ the noise hit amplitude. Fig.\ \ref{fig:SiPM-NoiseSpectrum} shows a measured \ac{SiPM} noise amplitude spectrum which follows the discussed behavior.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{sipm_noise.pdf}
    \caption{Example of the measured \ac{SiPM} noise rate for different thresholds. The noise rate decreases exponentially in steps, which correspond to one additional \ac{GAPD} being triggered by the initial noise event due to crosstalk. The distance between the steps is estimated to be around $\SI{4}{\milli\volt}$, which corresponds to the amplitude of one \ac{p.e.} unit \cite[adapted]{BSc-Ehlert}.}
    \label{fig:SiPM-NoiseSpectrum}
\end{figure}
\newline When reading out a scintillation detector with an \ac{SiPM}, one usually uses a discriminator with a threshold of several \ac{p.e.}, in order to suppress lower-order noise hits. Of course this also implies that the real event is required to have sufficient amount of photons to trigger the respective number of cells \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.

\section{Modern readout electronics}
\label{sec:ReadoutElectronics}
Since a major part of the work for this thesis was the development of an electronic readout system for a particle detector, this section should give an introduction into this topic. Emphasis is put on \acf{FPGA} devices which provide a suitable hardware basis for synchronous, fast readout systems. 

\subsection{General aspects}
\label{sec:ReadoutElectronics-General}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Quantity to read out: E.g. voltage / current
%    \item Discrimination of hits by amplitude
%    \item Good time resolution

%\end{itemize}
While early particle detectors were based on photographic or human readout \enquote{by eye}, most modern detectors use electronic readout systems. Apart from the fact that electronic readout is suited for high particle rates which greatly exceed the range possible with the human eye, it allows integration of the detector directly into a \ac{DAQ} system, which automatically processes the data and stores them in a digital format \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. Fig.\ \ref{fig:ReadOut-Schematic} shows an example detector readout chain with the electronic components that are described in the following paragraphs.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{ReadOut-Schematic.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic example of an electronic readout chain of a particle detector. The detector output signal is shaped and amplified by the preamplifier. After that, several components can be connected. The discriminator outputs a digital signal if the detector signal is above a certain threshold. The digital signal can then be converted to a timestamp with a \ac{TDC}. Alternatively, the analog signal can be digitized with an \ac{ADC} \cite[adapted]{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.}
    \label{fig:ReadOut-Schematic}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Frontend electronics}
The so-called frontend electronics receive the output signals from the detector itself. Detector outputs usually have the form of a charge, current or voltage. Since their amplitude is often very small, amplification of the signal is required. For this purpose, frontend electronics frequently include a so-called preamplifier, which can amplify the signals as well as convert them into a voltage if necessary \cite{Book-Leo}. Preamplifiers should have low noise rates and behave linearly. Often, there are additional filtering elements in the frontend electronics which provide pulse shaping. Depending on the detector type and application, the frontend electronics either output analog pulses or may include additional components to already convert them into digital signals \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Signal discrimination}
Usually, one implements a discrimination stage in the electronics chain. A discriminator has a digital output, that is, it converts the analog input pulse into a digital signal (which has the binary states low or high and no amplitude information anymore). Only when an input pulse with an amplitude above a defined threshold amplitude is present at the input, the digital output of the discriminator is switched to high. If one assumes that noise hits are typically of lower amplitudes, employing a discrimination stage can effectively suppress these hits. Discriminator outputs are used either to trigger other parts of the electronic readout chain or as direct part of the \ac{DAQ} chain \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
%In order to distinguish real signals from noise,
\paragraph{Amplitude measurement}
Discriminators only provide binary information whether an input signal was above a set threshold amplitude or not. Many applications however require the measurement of the signal amplitude, e.g.\ energy measurements. Since modern \ac{DAQ} systems operate digitally, it is necessary to convert the analog amplitude into a digital form, which is done by \acfp{ADC}. These devices convert an input signal within a defined amplitude range into a digital output value, which contains the amplitude information with respect to a given digitization step size which is typically in the order $\mathcal{O}(0.1-1\;\si{\milli\volt})$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Time measurement}
In many applications, the measurement and digitization of the arrival time of the signal is also required. This is especially critical in so-called \ac{ToF} measurements where particle propagation times are directly measured. For drift chambers, an accurate time measurement is also important, since the measured drift time yields the position of the initial particle hit (sec.\ \ref{sec:GaseousDetectors-DriftChamber}). A device which performs conversion of the timing of a hit into a digital format is called \acf{TDC}. Achievable time resolutions can be below $\mathcal{O}(\SI{100}{\pico\second})$ for advanced applications \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Data processing}
After signal amplification, shaping and discrimination or digitization, the data from the detector is processed by the \ac{DAQ}. The resulting high data rate may present a challenge for the readout system. One important concept is the so-called trigger, which decides whether the detector data from this event is stored or not. While simple triggers may only be the output of a discriminator which triggers the conversion of an \ac{ADC}, modern readout systems feature elaborated trigger algorithms where data is being processed faster than \SI{1}{\micro\second} and event readout is decided based on the computation result \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. Usually, the analysis of the recorded data is done later \enquote{offline} with computer systems, however few steps may also be computed already \enquote{online} by the \ac{DAQ}, meaning at the time of data recording.
\paragraph{Slow control}
Apart from signal readout, particle detectors often require infrastructure to operate, such as low-voltage, high-voltage, gas or cooling systems. Signals to control this periphery are often referred to as \enquote{slow control}, as these signals are usually exchanged at lower data rates than the signal readout \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Synchronization}
For increasingly complex detectors and readout electronics, the synchronization of the full system becomes nontrivial. As soon as the system has several detector channels and multiple readout devices, this is a relevant problem. It must be ensured that the recorded signals from all detector channels can be assigned to the correct event. Note that synchronization has additional weight at collider experiments, where the detector readout should be time-aligned with the particle collisions produced by the accelerator \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.

\subsection{FPGA devices}
\label{sec:ReadoutElectronics-FPGAs}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Brief history of FPGAs
%    \item Fully programmable logic (difference to CPU)
%    \item In particle physics: Fast, synchronized readout and processing of many channels
%\end{itemize}
\acfp{FPGA} are devices which are fully programmable to realize user-defined logic circuits. In contrast to this, microprocessors and microcontrollers have a fixed internal structure which may impose limitations in certain applications \cite{PhD-Eliseev}. The introduction of the \ac{FPGA} by Xilinx$^\text{TM}$ in 1984 revolutionized the field of programmable logic elements, featuring a new architecture which overcame the scalability limits of existing devices \cite{FPGA-History}.
\paragraph{Main components}
An \ac{FPGA} consists of a matrix of \acp{CLB}, wired by programmable interconnections. Each \ac{CLB} consists of three main components: A \ac{LUT}, a \ac{FF} and a multiplexer. The \ac{LUT} represents one logic gate with a programmable truth table and is intrinsically asynchronous. The \ac{FF} allows storage of the \ac{LUT} result and operates synchronously with a clock signal. The multiplexer connects the signals inside the \ac{CLB}. Typically, an \ac{FPGA} also features dedicated routes and elements for clock signals. Apart from \acp{CLB}, there are often some blocks which are not programmable themselves but serve frequently used functions (such as multiplication logic). One refers to these specialized blocks as hardware primitives or simply \enquote{hard blocks}. All internal resources of the device are collectively referred to as \ac{FPGA} fabric. On the chip edges, \acp{IOB} are located, which control the physical pins of the device and allow interfacing to periphery \cite{PhD-Eliseev}. Fig.\ \ref{fig:FPGA-Structure} schematically illustrates the internal structure of an \ac{FPGA} device.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{FpgaResources.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of the internal structure of an \ac{FPGA}. The fabric consists of a matrix of \acp{CLB} with programmable interconnections. \acp{IOB} on the chip edges allow interfacing to the external pins \cite{PhD-Eliseev}.}
    \label{fig:FPGA-Structure}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Application in particle physics}
For fast and synchronous signal acquisition of many channels of a particle detector as well as the execution of trigger algorithms, custom logic circuits are required. Regular microprocessors are usually not sufficient for such tasks, since they can only execute operations sequentially and can not always ensure deterministic signal propagation times. \acp{FPGA} do not only allow the implementation of custom logic and ensure deterministic behavior, but also offer the advantage of programmability. This is also the main advantage of \acp{FPGA} over customly fabricated chips, also called \acp{ASIC}, which have the logic circuit permanently \enquote{engraved} on them. For these reasons, \acp{FPGA} are commonly used in trigger systems of particle physics experiments and since recent times also in the signal acquisition electronics (cf.\ sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-Subdetectors}).

\subsection{Basics of logic circuit development}
\label{sec:ReadoutElectronics-LogicCircuits}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Description of logic circuitry implemented on FPGA
%    \item Combinatorial logic
%    \item Sequential / clocked logic
%    \item Hardware Description Language
%    \item IP cores
%\end{itemize}
The development of the readout electronics in this thesis also implied the development of the logic to be deployed on the readout system's \ac{FPGA}. Before the functionality of the developed firmware will be described in detail (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware}), general aspects of logic development should be briefly introduced in this section.
\paragraph{Combinatorial logic} A signal in digital logic is binary, i.e.\ it has only two states: \texttt{1} (high) or \texttt{0} (low). Digital circuits evaluate these signals to generate output signals based on the inputs. Combinatorial logic describes the type of circuits whose output only depends on the current input values. This means that combinatorial logic circuits do not include storage elements or a clock. A combinatorial circuit can be solely described with a \ac{LUT}, which includes all possibilities of input states and defines the respective output values \cite{FPGA-LogicCircuitsVHDL}.
\paragraph{Sequential logic}
Sequential logic circuits enable the realization of more complex functionalities. The outputs of a sequential logic circuit are not only allowed to depend on the current, but also on past input states. In order to store previous input states and internal signals, sequential logic employs storage elements and clock signals \cite{FPGA-LogicCircuitsVHDL}.
\paragraph{Logic circuit description}
In principle, all logic circuits can be described with logic diagrams that can be drawn \enquote{by hand}. However, circuits soon become large and complex to oversee. A \ac{HDL} overcomes this issue by using a text-based language to describe the logic circuitry. The standardized description of circuits with a \ac{HDL} allows the usage of common computer tools for logic simulation and development. The two dominant languages in use today are called Verilog and \ac{VHDL}, the latter of which was used for logic development in this work \cite{FPGA-LogicCircuitsVHDL}.
\newline Units of logic circuitry which serve a specific function are frequently packaged into so-called \acp{IP core}, which allows their sale, exchange, and reuse within a variety of logic development tools.
\paragraph{FPGA design flow}
The first step is always to formulate requirements on the logic circuits and then design them. This usually means writing the logic circuit in a text-based hierarchical \ac{HDL}, but may also include interconnecting logic blocks in a visual interface called \enquote{block design}. After the circuit is defined, the next step is synthesis of the design. In this step, the logic circuit is simplified as far as possible and translated into a list of building blocks contained in the \ac{FPGA}. However, the exact mapping and interconnections on the chip is not defined yet. This is done in the last step referred to as implementation, which also takes into account the user-defined pin mapping by constraining logic signals to the respective \acp{IOB}. The placement of the logic on the chip is calculated to respect the timing constraints given by the clock frequencies and gate response times \cite{PhD-Eliseev}.
