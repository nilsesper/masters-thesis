Understanding particle interactions with matter is essential when constructing and operating particle detectors. This is because detectors are never sensitive to elementary particles themselves, but to the signatures that the particles leave in the detector through interactions with the detector material \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\newline The topic of particle interactions is quite rich, as different particles can undergo different interactions and the interaction types depend on the particle energy. Since in this thesis a muon detector is discussed, the topic is restricted to interactions of heavy charged particles (which include the muon). Particles passing through matter interact with it in various ways and thereby lose energy. A quantity commonly used in this context is the mean particle energy loss per path length in the material $-\left\langle dE/dx \right\rangle$ which also called stopping power \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}\footnote{Since the interactions also depend on the material density $\rho$, one usually weights the path length $l$ with this quantity: $x=l\rho$. This gives the stopping power the unit $\si{\mega\electronvolt \; \centi\meter^2 \gram^{-1}}$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.}.

\subsection{Ionization and excitation}
\label{sec:Interactions-Ionization}
Charged particles can ionize and excite atoms of the medium thereby losing energy. This effect is the dominant process of energy loss for charged particles, until radiative effects become relevant at high energies (sec.\ \ref{sec:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung}).
\paragraph{Stopping power calculation}
Generally, the stopping power can be described with an integral over all possible energy transfers $T$ for the collisions between the charged particle and the shell electrons \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}:
\begin{equation}
    -\left\langle \frac{dE}{dx} \right\rangle = n_e\int_{T_\text{min}}^{T_\text{max}} T \; \frac{d\sigma_a}{dT}(M,\beta,T)\;dT.
    \label{eq:Interactions-Integral}
\end{equation}
Here, $n_e$ is the target electron density and $T_\text{min}$ ($T_\text{max}$) the minimum (maximum) allowed energy transfer. The differential cross section $d\sigma_a/dT(M,\beta,T)$ is a measure of the probability of the collision process of given energy transfer $T$ for a particle with mass $M$ and velocity $\beta=v/c$ ($c=\SI{2.998e8}{\meter/\second}$ is the speed of light \cite{PDG2024}). Respecting the fact that the minimum energy transfer can not be arbitrarily small in due to the quantized electron energy levels in the atom and allowing approximations for heavy particles $M \gg m_e$ (which is the case for muons, see sec.\ \ref{sec:SM-Muon}), one obtains \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}
\[
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        T_\text{min}= \frac{I^2}{2m_e c^2\beta^2},
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        T_\text{max}= \frac{2 m_e c^2\beta^2\gamma^2}{1+2\gamma m_e/M+m_e^2/M^2},
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\]
where $I\approx 1.7 \cdot Z^{0.85} \;\si{\electronvolt}$ is the mean excitation energy of the atom with atomic number $Z$, $\gamma=1/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}$ is the Lorentz factor of the heavy particle and $m_e=\SI{511}{\kilo\electronvolt}$ the electron mass \cite{PDG2024}.
\paragraph{Bethe-Bloch formula}
Computing the integral from eq.\ \ref{eq:Interactions-Integral} with the Mott cross section (which takes into account the spin of both the electron and the heavy charged particle) yields the Bethe-Bloch formula for the stopping power \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}:
\begin{equation}
    -\left\langle \frac{dE}{dx} \right\rangle = K\frac{Z}{A}\frac{z^2}{\beta^2}\left[ \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{2m_ec^2\beta^2\gamma^2T_\text{max}}{I^2}-\beta^2-\frac{\delta(\beta\gamma)}{2}-\frac{C(\beta\gamma,\;I)}{Z} \right].
    \label{eq:Interactions-BetheBloch}
\end{equation}
Here, $A$ is the atomic mass number of the medium, $z$ the charge of the interacting heavy particle and $K= N_Ae^4/(4\pi \epsilon_0 m_e c^2)$ a factor including the Avogadro constant $N_A=\SI{6.022e23}{\mole^{-1}}$ and $\epsilon_0=\SI{8.854e-12}{\farad\meter^{-1}}$ the vacuum permittivity \cite{PDG2024}. The formula is able to describe the stopping power for heavy charged particles in the velocity range $0.1 \lesssim \beta\gamma \lesssim 1000$ \cite{PDG2024}. Generally, the stopping power curve behaves like $\propto 1/\beta^2$ for lower velocities and then reaches a minimum at around $\beta\gamma\approx 3.5$. Particles in this stopping power minimum are called \acp{MiP}. For higher velocities, the stopping power increases logarithmically $\propto\ln\gamma$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}.
\paragraph{Correction terms}
Eq.\ \ref{eq:Interactions-BetheBloch} contains two additional correction terms ($\delta$ and $C$) which should be discussed in the following.
\newline When the velocity of the heavy charged particle is as low as the shell electron velocities, the approximative description of the energy loss with the mean excitation energy is no longer valid, since quamtum mechanical interference effects start to become relevant. The stopping power deviates from the $\propto 1/\beta^2$ behavior and decreases again towards lower velocities. This so-called shell correction is described by the $C(\beta\gamma,\; I)/Z$ term and increases the validity range of eq.\ \ref{eq:Interactions-BetheBloch} to velocity ranges below $\beta\gamma < 0.1$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}.
\newline For high particle velocities, the stopping power also deviates from the $\propto\ln\gamma$ behavior, due to polarization effects of the material and the fact that the electric field changes its shape for relativistic particle velocities. The density correction $\delta(\beta\gamma)$ accounts for this effect, leading to a flattened rise of the stopping power curve \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}.
\paragraph{Statistics of energy loss}
The Bethe-Bloch formula (with its corrections) describes only the mean value of the energy loss per unit length, statistically the energy loss follows a Landau distribution. Larger energy transfers can lead to high-energy electrons, which are referred to as $\delta$-electrons \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}.

\subsection{Bremsstrahlung and other radiative losses}
\label{sec:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung}
According to electrodynamics, accelerated charges emit photon radiation. In the case of charged particles in matter, the electic field is the Coulomb field of the material atom's nucelei. The radiated photons are referred to as Bremsstrahlung and lead to energy loss of the deflected particle.
\paragraph{Bremsstrahlung for electrons}
The energy loss due to Bremsstrahlung becomes dominating for particles with high velocities $\beta\gamma \gtrsim 1000$ and can be described as follows \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}:
\begin{equation}
    -\left\langle \frac{dE}{dx} \right\rangle =-\frac{E}{X_0}.
    \label{eq:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung}
\end{equation}
Here, $X_0$ is the radiation length, i.e.\ the path after which a particle has only left $1/e$ of its initial energy due to the radiative energy loss. Calculation of the radiation length is extensive and usually includes approximations. From eq.\ \ref{eq:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung} is is clear that the particle energy decreases exponentially when radiating Bremsstrahlung photons \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}:
\begin{equation*}
    E(x)=E_0 \; e^{-x/X_0}.
\end{equation*}
\paragraph{Radiative losses for heavier particles}
Note that the description above is for electrons. Of course, heavy charged particles (such as muons) show radiative losses as well, however at even higher energies, since radiative losses are heavily dependent on the particle mass $dE/dx \propto 1/M^2$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. Apart from photon radiation, other effects such as $e^+e^-$ pair production taking place directly in the Coulomb field and photonuclear interactions have to be taken into account for such calculations \cite{Interactions-MuonStoppingPower}.
\paragraph{Critical energy}
The energy at which the radiative losses exceed the ionization losses is called critical energy and depends on the particle type as well as the material. Due to the mass scaling of radiative losses, the critical energy of electrons $E_c=\mathcal{O}(\SI{10}{\mega\electronvolt})$ is significantly smaller than the critical energy of muons $E_{\mu,c}=\mathcal{O}(\SI{100}{\giga\electronvolt})$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.

\subsection{Coulomb scattering}
\label{sec:Interactions-CoulombScattering}
Charged particles can experience Rutherford scattering on the Coulomb field of the atom's nucleus. Scattering is also possible in the field of the shell electrons, however this contribution is neglectable for high particle  energies $E\gg m_e c^2$. The Rutherford cross section can describe a single scattering process at angle $\theta$ for a particle with momentum $p$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}:
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} \propto z^2 Z^2 \frac{1}{p^2 \beta^2} \frac{1}{\sin^4\frac{\theta}{2}}.
\end{equation*}
\paragraph{Total scattering angle}
Several scattering processes occur when the particle passes through a material of thickness $x$. The total scattering angle distribution resulting from the consecutive scattering processes can be approximated with a Gaussian distribution $f(\theta)$ centered around $\theta=0$. The width $\theta_0$ of the distribution of scattering angles can be estimated with \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}
\begin{equation*}
    \theta_0 \approx \frac{\sqrt{2\pi} m_e c}{\sqrt{\alpha}} \frac{1}{p \beta} \sqrt{\frac{x}{X_0}}
\end{equation*}
where $X_0$ is the radiation length of the material (see sec.\ \ref{sec:Interactions-Bremsstrahlung}) and $\alpha \approx 1/137$ is the electromagnetic fine structure constant \cite{PDG2024}.

\subsection{Other effects}
\label{sec:Interactions-OtherEffects}
Charged particles can experience additional processes when passing through matter, which however are of less importance in the scope of this thesis. For completeness, they should still be mentioned here.
\paragraph{Cherenkov radiation}
Charged particles passing through matter with a velocity exceeding the velocity of light in the medium emit so-called Cherenkov photons. The energy loss due to this process is neglible, however some particle detectors exploit the process by detecting the emitted photons. The angle $\theta_c$ at which the photons are emitted in this process depends on the refraction index of the medium $n$ and the particle velocity $\beta$, allowing for velocity measurements of non-relativistic particles with this process \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}:
\begin{equation*}
    \cos\theta_c=\frac{1}{n\beta}.
\end{equation*}
\paragraph{Transition radiation}
Another effect is transition radiation, which implies the emission of photons when a charged particle crosses the boundary of different media. Also this process does not contribute significantly to energy loss. Note however that the radiated energy depends on the particle's Lorentz factor $\gamma$, allowing to exploit this effect for velocity measurements of relativistic particles \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,PDG2024}.


\section{Scintillators and photon detection}
\label{sec:Scintillators}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Scintillator: Photon emission due to energy deposition of particle
%    \item Sensitive photon detection: PMT, SiPM (discussed in detail)
%\end{itemize}
One of the two detectors used in this work is a scintillation detector. Therefore the working principles of scintillators as well as their readout should be briefly introduced in this section.

\subsection{Scintillators}
\label{sec:Scintillators-Scintillators}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Organic and anorganic scintillators
%    \item Plastic scintillator (that is used in this thesis)
%\end{itemize}
A scintillator is a material which emits (optical) photons when a passing particle deposits energy in it. The emitted light follows a characteristic spectrum caused by the material properties, one also speaks of luminescence \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. Scintillation detectors played an important role in the early days of particle physics, as they were used in the Rutherford scattering experiments of $\alpha$-particles with nuclei. While the scintillation light was initially observed with the bare eye, the invention of the \ac{PMT} and in more recent days the \ac{SiPM} (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-SiPMs}) allowed scintillators to be used in modern experiments \cite{Book-Leo}.
\paragraph{Scintillator properties}
Generally, a scintillator should exhibit a high efficiency for conversion of deposited energy into scintillation light, as well as a linearity between the number of scintillation photons and the deposited energy. Also the time between energy absorption and photon emission should be short in order to have a fast detector. Apart from this, the optical properties of the scintillator should be such that the scintillator is transparent for its own scintillation light and the optical coupling to the photon readout does not cause large losses \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Scintillator types}
One differentiates organic and inorganic scintillators by their material and scintillation mechanism. An inorganic scintillator consists of inorganic materials, which form a cristalline structure. The scintillation effect can be explained with the band structure of the electrons in the crystal. Energy deposition in the material can lead to excitation of an electron, which can then deexcite at a luminescence center in the crystal under photon emission. Usually these luminescence centers are impurities added to the crystal via doping \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,Book-Leo}.
\newline Organic scintillators on the other hand consist of organic materials, i.e.\ carbon compounds where the electronic structure of the molecules drives the fluorescent properties. Of highest relevance are the delocalized $\pi$-orbital electrons. Electrons become elevated to higher energy levels due to the energy deposition in the material and can deexcite radiatively, leading to scintillation light. A frequently used form of organic scintillators are so-called plastic scintillators, which embed the scintillation materials embedded in a solid plastic solvent \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,Book-Leo}.
\paragraph{Photon readout}
To achieve high photon detection efficiency, it is important to optimize the optical connection between the scintillator and the photon detector. A special emphasis should be put on material transitions in the optical path (ideally similar refractive indices) as well as the geometry to reduce photon loss. If the wavelength of the light emitted by scintillator deviates from the ideal wavelength for the photon detector, one can also employ wavelength shifting materials which are able to convert photon wavelengths \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,Book-Leo}. There exist several devices for optical readout with (nearly) single-photon sensitivity, among them \acp{PMT} and \acp{SiPM}. The latter should be discussed in more detail in the following section, as it is used in the detector presented in this work.

\subsection{Silicon photomultipliers}
\label{sec:Scintillators-SiPMs}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Geiger-Avalance Photodiode array
%    \item Sensitivity and performance
%    \item Output signal shape
%    \item Characteristic noise spectrum
%\end{itemize}
\acfp{SiPM} are semiconductor devices capable of detecting single photons in the optical spectrum. Even though their name hints a similarity to \acp{PMT} which were mostly used until recent times, they do not share the main characteristics. While \acp{PMT} rely on a photocathode for photon-to-electron conversion (exploiting the photoelectric effect) and a vacuum tube housing a high-voltage electron amplification assembly to amplify the signal of single photoelectrons, the \ac{SiPM} is purely a solid state device \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{$p$-$n$-junction}
A diode consists of a junction of a $p$- and $n$-doped semiconductor. Doping means that defect atoms are added into the semiconductor lattice which increase the concentration of electrons ($n$-doping) or holes\footnote{Positive charges in the lattice are called holes and effectively describe a missing electron in a lattice atom.} ($p$-doping). At the $p$-$n$-junction, charge carriers drift towards the oppositely doped region because of the different densities of electrons and holes. Due to the oppositely acting electric field created by the charge displacement, a force equillibrium is reached. The result is a so-called depletion zone at the junction without any charge carriers and an intrinsic electric field. Usually one applies a reverse bias voltage to the junction, increasing the depletion zone width and electric field strength \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,Book-Leo}.
\paragraph{Geiger-Avalance photodiode}
Photons can excite electrons from the valence into the conduction band, which is referred to as internal photoeffect.\footnote{In contrast, the external photoeffect describes the ionization of atom, i.e.\ freeing of an electron from an atom, due to photon absorption. This usually takes place when the photon energy is higher.} The excitation results in an electron-hole pair in the semiconductor. In the depletion zone, the charges are separated and drift in opposite directions due to the electric field. The resulting current can be measured, making the device a photodiode capable of detecting optical photons \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\newline Modifications in the semiconductor doping profile as well as higher doping concentration enable amplification of the electron and hole currents (which were created by the arriving photon) in the depletion region. When applying high enough reverse bias voltages, the photodiode becomes a \acf{GAPD}. Its amplification $>\mathcal{O}(10^{5})$ is so strong that the depletion zone breaks down (i.e.\ becomes conductive) once one electron-hole-pair was created in the depletion region. This gives the \ac{GAPD} the capability of single-photon detection. A resistor is needed to quench the discharge in order to "rearm" the device. The name "Geiger" is chosen in analogy to the respective operation mode of gaseous detectors which exhibit similar behavior (see sec.\ \ref{sec:GaseousDetectors-OperationModes}) \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{SiPM device}
An \ac{SiPM} consists of an array of $\mathcal{O}(10^{3})$ \acp{GAPD} and their respective quenching resistors integrated onto one semiconductor die. Advantages of the diode array compared to a single larger-area diode are the reduced noise rate as well as the reduction of dead time effects, since not the full detector but only the hit array cell is disabled during the dead time. However the array structure leads to an additional effect, namely the possibility of crosstalk between cells. An additional effect are afterpulses which occurs due to charge carriers being trapped during the amplification avelance and only being released at a later time \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,SiPM-Gundacker}.
\newline Since the diodes operate in Geiger mode, the signal amplitude is approximately similar for each single hit cell. This means that the resulting signal from the \ac{SiPM} is discrete in amplitude. One refers to the signal amplitude of one cell as \acf{p.e.} \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,SiPM-Gundacker}.
\newline {\color{red} Add schematic drawing and photo of SiPM}
\paragraph{Thermal noise}
As a semiconductor detector, the \ac{SiPM} is subject to thermal noise. Thermally excited electrons in the sensitive region of a diode lead to triggering of one cell and thus a noise hit. In principle, the noise hits of all cells are independent. Taking into account the probability for crosstalk however, the noise spectrum of an \ac{SiPM} does showcase noise hits with several \ac{p.e.} amplitude.\footnote{Of course it is (in principle) also possible for noise hits of two cells in the \ac{SiPM} to happen independently within a short amount of time.} The frequency of multiple \ac{p.e.} noise hits compared to single cell noise hits allows estimation of the crosstalk probability of the device \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes,SiPM-Gundacker}. Note that for each additional hit cell due to crosstalk, the crosstalk probability needs to be added as a factor to the expected rate. Therefore one expects an exponentially falling behavior of the noise rate w.r.t.\ the noise hit amplitude.
\newline Usually, one sets a threshold af several \ac{p.e.} for hit discrimination, in order to suppress lower-order noise hits. Of course this also implies that the real event is required to have sufficient amount of photons to trigger the respective number of cells \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.

\section{Gaseous detectors}
\label{sec:GaseousDetectors}
The second detector type used in this thesis is a gaseous detector, more specifically a drift chamber. Gaseous detectors rely on ionization (similar to scintillators, see sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators}), however the detector material is a gas. A passing particle loses energy in the gas while ionizing gas atoms, creating electon-ion-pairs. When applying an electric field to the gas volume, the created charges are separated and a current is measured \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Brief history of gaseous detectors}
Gaseous detectors have a long history in particle physics, marking the first step from scintillation detectors read out manually "by eye" (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-Scintillators}) towards detectors producing electronic signals \cite{GaseousDetectors-HilkeRiegler}. The first gaseous detector was described by Rutherford and Geiger in 1908 and was able to detect $\alpha$-particles from a radioactive source \cite{GaseousDetectors-RutherfordGeiger}. The Geiger-Müller tube introduced in 1928 showcased much stronger gas amplification \cite{GaseousDetectors-GeigerMüller}. The introduction of \ac{MWPC} by Charpak in 1968 opened new application fields of gaseous detectors \cite{GaseousDetectors-Charpak}. The discovery of the $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons at the $Sp\bar{p}S$ collider at \acs{CERN} in 1983 \cite{W-Z-Discovery} was (in part) enabled by the UA-1 detector \cite{UA1-Main} consisting of a gaseous drift chamber for tracking and drift tube muon chambers.\footnote{The UA-1 muon drift chambers, which played a crucial role in the boson discoveries, were developed at Physics Institute III A in Aachen \cite{UA1-Faissner}.} While semiconductor detectors nowadays showcase better energy and spatial resolution and have therefore replaced gaseous detectors in particle tracking detectors in many cases, gaseous detectors continue to be used in numerous applications. This is especially the case for applications which require large detector areas, where gaseous detectors captivate with reasonable prices \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. An example for this are the muon systems of both the \acs{CMS} \cite{CMS-Main} and \acs{ATLAS} \cite{ATLAS-Main} experiments at \acs{CERN} which consist solely of gaseous detectors. Also, research in the field of gaseous detectors is ongoing, which already led to novel high-performance micropattern gaseous detectors technologies \cite{GaseousDetectors-HilkeRiegler}.

\subsection{Operation modes of gaseous detectors}
\label{sec:GaseousDetectors-OperationModes}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Ionization chamber
%    \item Proportional mode
%    \item Nonlinear amplification mode
%    \item Geiger mode
%\end{itemize}
Depending on the voltage applied to the detector and the resulting field strength, the gas detector operated in different modes, which should be described in this section.
\newline {\color{red} Add gaseous detector operation modes plot}
\paragraph{Recombination region}
When no voltage or very low voltages are applied to the gas detector, the electron-ion-pairs created by the passing ionizing particle can recombine before they can be collected by the readout electrodes \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}. This mode is therefore of no use for a particle detector. 
\paragraph{Ionization chamber}
For medium voltages, the primary electron-ion-pairs are collected by the electrodes. Since the energy loss of particles in gases is rather low, the number of collected charges per particle is also small.\footnote{In argon, a \ac{MiP} generates only $\mathcal{O}(100)$ electron-ion-pairs per \si{\centi\meter}, equivalent to created charges in the \si{\femto\coulomb} range which are (almost) impossible to detect without amplification \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.} Therefore ionization chambers are rather suited for particle flux measurements than for single-particle detection \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Proportional region}
When the electric field strengths become larger with increasing voltages, the effect of gas amplification starts to play a role. The drifting electrons gain sufficient energy to ionize gas atoms themselves, leading to secondary electron-ion-pairs which themselves can again trigger ionization processes. For a wide voltage range, the resulting charge collected at the electrode remains proportional to the initially deposited charge. The gain $G$ is defined as ration of initial and final number of charge carriers and reaches values of $G\approx 10^3-10^5$ in the proportional region \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Region of limited proportionality}
When the voltage is further increased, the proportionality between initial and final number of charges becomes invalid. This is due to space charge effects, i.e.\ clouds of slower moving ions which shield the electric field \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Geiger region}
In the Geiger region, the output signal becomes independent of the number of primary charges. Such a detector can be used for particle counting purposes, however the ability to estimate the particle's energy loss with the deposited charge due to ionization is lost. In the Geiger mode, the gas discharge is not confined towards the electrode, but extends over the whole gas volume. Such a detector needs to be quenched (i.e.\ the discharge needs to be stopped) with an external resistor in order to become sensitive again to detect the next particle. The amplification in this mode can reach values of $G\approx 10^8-10^9$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Discharge region}
When the voltage is increased even beyond the Geiger region, gas discharges occur where both electrodes become connected via a plasma channel. This discharge can be triggered by ionizing particles or spontaneously. With measures to suppress the complete gas discharge, this mode can also be used for a particle detector, e.g.\ in so-called streamer chambers \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.

\subsection{Drift chamber}
\label{sec:GaseousDetectors-DriftChamber}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Proportional mode
%    \item Constant electric field and drift velocity
%    \item Correlation of charge travelling time and initial ionization position
%    \item Drift chamber legacy in Aachen
%\end{itemize}
A drift chamber is a gaseous detector which not only allows detection of ionizing particles, but enables estimations of the traversing particle's position within the gas volume. It is usually operated in proportional mode, however the amplification region where the gas amplification takes place is localized and there exists a significant volume where the created charges drift without any amplification. They can be seen as further development of the \ac{MWPC} detector type. Drift chambers are famously used in muon detection systems which usually are located on the outer shell of a general-purpose particle detector experiment and therefore require large detector areas while still demanding good spatial resolution \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\newline {\color{red} Add picture of drift chamber and drift cell}
\paragraph{Drift region}
The drift region is considered the sensitive region of the detector and usually comprises most of its volume. When a passing particle creates electon-ion-pairs in this region, the charges are separated through an applied electric field. In the drift region, the field strengths are not high enough to trigger gas amplification. Instead, the charges drift with a constant drift velocity $v_D$, experiencing a force equillibrium between the electic force and a decellarating force due to scattering processes with other gas atoms. Note that this drift velocity depends on the electic field, the gas mixture as well as the particle type.\footnote{The drift velocity of electrons is significantly higher than the velocity of ions \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.} Typical drift velocities for drift chambers lie in the range $\mathcal{O}(\SI{50}{\micro\meter/\nano\second})$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\newline At some point the drifting charges reach the electrode and are collected. One should note that the drift with a known velocity $v_D$ allows to estimate the drift distance of the charges and thus the initial position of the passing particle \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}:
\begin{equation*}
    x_D = \int_{t_\text{start}}^{t_\text{stop}}v_D(t)\;dt.
\end{equation*}
This is possible with only a time measurement, which is however required to have good resolution $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\nano\second})$ if resolutions of $\mathcal{O}(\SI{100}{\micro\meter})$ should be reached. Note that a strong advantage of drift chambers is a spatial resolution which significantly exceeds the granularity of the readout channels. Ideally, the drift velocity is constant over the full width of the drift region, resulting in a linear space-time relation. This requires design efforts to make the electric field in the drift region as homogeneous as possible \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
\paragraph{Gas amplification} Drift chambers usually have an anode wire for electron collection. Usually, the field strengths close to the wire are strong enough to enable the amplification of the arriving electrons, thereby increasing the output signal amplitude. Usually the drift and amplification regions are not strictly separated, as in the described example of the anode wire. This leads to non-linearities in the space-time relation, which create the need for calibrations \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.
