\label{sec:Scint}
This chapter focuses on the second muon detector used in this thesis: A scintillation strip detector with \ac{SiPM} photon readout. The readout electronics and firmware were newly developed for this work, with the intention to connect the readout to the Phase-2 \ac{DT} backend (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-Phase2Readout}). The goal is to provide synchronous operation of the scintillator and the \ac{DT} chamber with this system (sec.\ \ref{sec:Combined}).

\section{Detector setup}
\label{sec:Scint-Setup}
%Mention self-development of detector at institute \cite{BSc-Ehlert} \cite{MSc-Ehlert}.
The detector itself was developed at Physics Institute III A in Aachen over the course of past Bachelor's and Master's theses \cite{BSc-Ehlert,MSc-Ehlert}. The development of the detector was already completed as this thesis began. The device is only briefly described here, as details on the design and construction can be found in the documents cited above.
\paragraph{Detector layout}
The detector consists of two layers of 16 scintillator strips stacked with perpendicular orientation to each other, each measuring \SI{50}{\centi\meter} in length, \SI{3}{\centi\meter} in width, and \SI{0.5}{\centi\meter} in thickness. The assembly gives a sensitive detector area of around $50 \times 50 \;\si{\centi\meter^2}$ divided into $16 \times 16 = 256$ quadratic sectors with a side length of $\SI{3}{\centi\meter}$. The detector material itself is an organic plastic scintillator (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-Scintillators}) referred to as EJ-200 \cite{Scint-PlasticScint}. The strips are wrapped in Tyvek\textsuperscript{\textregistered} 1442R reflective fabric \cite{Scint-Tyvek}. Light-guiding and wavelength-shifting fibers are glued into both long edges of each scintillator strip. The casing of the detector not only provides light isolation from external photons, but also includes walls in between each of the strips, to suppress optical crosstalk between layers \cite{BSc-Ehlert,MSc-Ehlert}. Fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-Setup-Renderings} shows two renderings of the detector.

\paragraph{Photon detection}
The photons created in the scintillator are detected by 64 \acp{SiPM} (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-SiPMs}). One \ac{SiPM} is mounted on both ends of each strip, reading out the photons from the two wavelength-shifting fibers. The \ac{SiPM} model used is the Onsemi\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} J-Series 30035 \cite{Scint-OnsemiSiPM}. The optical coupling between the scintillator and the \ac{SiPM} is accomplished with ELASTOSIL\textsuperscript{\textregistered} RT 604 optical silicon rubber pads \cite{Scint-Elastosil}.

\section{Frontend electronics}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item SiPM front-end board with preamplifier
%    \item Backbone of detector with bias voltage supply
%    \item Raspberry Pi for backbone control
%\end{itemize}
\label{sec:Scint-Frontend}
\paragraph{Signal preamplifier} A small frontend board is mounted directly at the scintillator. The \ac{SiPM} itself is mounted on this \ac{PCB}. Additionally, it features a preamplifier with an amplification of $+22\;\si{\decibel}$, which amplifies the  \ac{SiPM} output signal to measurable voltage ranges. With the applied bias voltage of \SI{29}{\volt}, the setup achieves an output amplitude for one \ac{p.e.} (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-SiPMs}) of around $\SI{5}{\milli\volt}$ \cite{MSc-Ehlert}. In addition, the \ac{PCB} hosts a \ac{LED} which may be used to test the photon readout. However, in this work, this feature is not used. Fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-Setup-FrontendPCB} shows a picture of one frontend \ac{PCB}.
\paragraph{Frontend infrastructure}
Around the sides of the detector, a total of eight so-called \enquote{backbone} \acp{PCB} are mounted. Each backbone board directly connects to eight frontend \acp{PCB}, i.e.\ eight \acp{SiPM}. It supplies the bias voltage as well as the operation voltage for the preamplifier. The amplified signals are provided on the backbone \ac{PCB} via \ac{MCX} connectors, in order to be further transmitted to the readout system via coaxial cables. A Raspberry Pi \cite{Raspberry-Pi} is used to control the bias voltage levels of all channels \cite{MSc-Ehlert}.

\paragraph{Operating conditions}
Due to fabrication tolerances, the internal breakdown voltage of the individual \acp{SiPM} vary as much as \SI{0.5}{\volt} for the used model \cite{Scint-OnsemiSiPM}. As the \ac{SiPM} output amplitude varies with the applied bias voltage, one can, in principle, correct for these fluctuations between \acp{SiPM} by conducting extensive calibration measurements \cite{MSc-Ehlert}. However, measurements in this work do not aim at the characterization of the detector performance or the precision determination of the cosmic muon rate. Therefore, these high-precision measurements were omitted, and a constant bias voltage of \SI{29}{\volt} was set.

To read out the preamplified \ac{SiPM} hits, new readout electronics and firmware have been developed in this thesis, which are presented in the following. These electronics are designed to be used together with the \ac{DT} backend system, to integrate the \ac{SiPM}-based scintillator synchronously into the \ac{DT} chamber readout.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard-Overview}
\paragraph{General requirements}
The following requirements were defined for the readout board: It should feature 32 input channels for analog \ac{SiPM} signals. These signals should be discriminated with a given threshold voltage (cf.\ sec.\ \ref{sec:ReadoutElectronics-General}). A timestamp should be assigned to each hit, with a precision of $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\nano\second})$. For calibration and testing purposes, there should also be the capability to send simultaneous testpulses to all input channels. The board should operate synchronously with a central clock, assign global timestamps to all recorded hits, and transfer them to the \ac{DT} backend. The compatibility with the \ac{DT} backend is an essential feature, as it is the main goal of this work to interface the \ac{DT} readout chain.
\paragraph{Readout board}
A new \ac{PCB} was developed to host the readout system according to the requirements listed above. Fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-Readout-CarrierBoard} shows a picture of the board. It features 32 \ac{MCX} connectors for the \ac{SiPM} input signals. 
\par The hit data is processed with an AMD\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} Xilinx Zynq\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} UltraScale+\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} MPSoC module \cite{UltraScale-FPGA}. A \ac{SoC} is a chip that features both \ac{PL}, i.e.\ an \ac{FPGA} (sec.\ \ref{sec:ReadoutElectronics-FPGAs}), and a \ac{PS}, i.e.\ one or several microprocessor cores. They allow combining the advantages of \acp{FPGA} with conventional computing and offer fast data interfaces between both units. The particular device used is the Trenz Elektronic TE-0820 module, which features an AMD\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} Xilinx XCZU3EG-1SFVC784E \ac{SoC} \cite{FPGA-Trenz}.
\par To forward the acquired data to the \ac{DT} backend and to receive the central clock, another \ac{PCB} was developed, the \ac{lpGBT} mezzanine, which is described in more detail in sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-lpGBTMezzanine}. The readout board hosts an \ac{FMC} connector to plug the mezzanine onto it.

\subsection{Discrimination with FPGA-internal comparators}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Comparators for input signal discrimination, however no conventional, dedicated hardware but FPGA-internal comparators used.
%    \item Use FPGA-internal blocks for deserialization to increase time resolution
%\end{itemize}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item FPGA-internal comparators (intended for differential signalling)
%    \item Novel concept: Use FPGA-internal comparators with analog signals \cite{FPGA-MVT-Xi} \cite{FPGA-MVT-Eliseev}
%    \item Voltage limits of inputs
%    \item Deserializers for higher time resolution
%\end{itemize}
\label{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard-FPGASampling}
As described above (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard-Overview}), the amplified analog \ac{SiPM} signals should be discriminated with an adjustable threshold to convert them to a digital signal. To implement a discriminator in electronics, one usually uses a comparator. While it is possible to buy and use such comparators as discrete electronic components, in this thesis, a different approach is used.
\paragraph{FPGA IOB resources}
The \acfp{IOB} of modern \acp{FPGA} also feature comparators (sec.\ \ref{sec:ReadoutElectronics-FPGAs}). The comparators have the purpose of converting differential two-wire digital signals at the chip inputs to single-ended signals used in the \ac{FPGA} fabric itself. In the case of AMD\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} Xilinx Zynq\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} UltraScale+\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} \acp{FPGA}, the hardware primitive which offers this capability is called \texttt{IBUFDS} \cite{UltraScale-FPGA-SelectIO-Resources}.
\paragraph{Analog signal sampling}
Our group is among the first to employ these \ac{FPGA}-internal comparator resources to sample analog signals \cite{FPGA-MVT-Eliseev,FPGA-MVT-Xi}. An open-source \ac{IP core} has been developed by our group (preceding this work), which offers \ac{ADC} capabilities using this method \cite{FPGA-MVT-Eliseev}.
\par Using the \ac{FPGA}-internal comparators for sampling can offer several advantages: First, the input circuitry is simplified by eliminating the need for discrete comparators. Furthermore, the \ac{FPGA}-internal comparators offer fast response times, as they are intended to be used for high-speed digital signals. It should be noted that the \ac{FPGA} \acp{IOB} do provide additional hardware primitives which may be useful in the context of signal sampling, such as deserializers, which can be used as \acp{TDC} (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-SamplingTDC}).
%On the negative side, it is clear that twice the amount of input pins is required to perform analog signal sampling, in comparison to using discrete comparators and only using one single-ended \ac{FPGA} input pin.
\paragraph{Limitations and challenges}
The \ac{FPGA}-internal comparators are not specified to operate with analog signals, but only with differential digital signals. Therefore, the injected analog signals to be sampled need to obey several requirements: The \ac{LVDS} comparator inputs require a DC voltage offset. Here, a value of $\approx\SI{900}{\milli\volt}$ is chosen, which is within the electrical requirements. This offset comes as the DC-part of the analog input signal (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard-InputCircuitry}). The input voltage at the comparator pins should not exceed $\SI{1.4}{\volt}$ \cite{UltraScale-FPGA-SelectIO-Switching}.
\par Due to fluctuations during manufacturing, the comparators do not behave ideally, but exhibit a switching bias of up to $\pm 35\;\si{\milli\volt}$ \cite{UltraScale-FPGA-SelectIO-Resources}, which may be different for each individual input. This bias needs to be calibrated before the comparators can be used (sec.\ \ref{sec:ScintData-VoltageBias}).

\subsection{Analog input circuitry}

\label{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard-InputCircuitry}
The analog circuitry on the readout board should perform the input signal conditioning as well as the generation of synchronous testpulses for all inputs. Fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-ReadoutBoard-AnalogCircuit} shows the schematic of the realized circuit for one \ac{SiPM} input.
\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{analog_input_circuit.pdf}
    \caption{Schematic of analog input circuitry of one \ac{SiPM} input channel. The pre-amplified pulse enters on the left. The DC offset of the signal is provided at $V_\text{com}$, and the discriminator trigger level (including the DC offset) is provided at $V_\text{thres}$. On the right, the signals $V_\pm$ are routed to the \ac{FPGA}-internal comparator, which is not shown in this schematic, as the comparators already lie inside the \ac{FPGA} chip.}
    \label{fig:Scint-ReadoutBoard-AnalogCircuit}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{SiPM input}
The \ac{SiPM} signal is received at the \ac{MCX} connector. As explained, the \ac{FPGA} internal comparators require a DC offset voltage of $V_\text{com}\approx\SI{900}{\milli\volt}$. This voltage is commonly provided for all \ac{SiPM} input channels. The \ac{SiPM} input is connected through a serial capacitor. In this way, the AC-component of the \ac{SiPM} pulse is added to the $V_\text{com}$ offset in the circuit. The resulting signal is connected to the positive comparator input $V_+$.
\par The threshold voltage $V_\text{thres}$ for hit discrimination of the input signal is supplied to the negative input $V_-$ of the comparator. The threshold voltage can be set individually for each input with a \ac{DAC}.
\paragraph{Testpulse generation}
One of the \ac{FPGA} pins is configured to issue a digital synchronous testpulse signal. This testpulse signal is received simultaneously by all comparator inputs and therefore allows the timing calibration of all inputs relative to each other (sec.\ \ref{sec:ScintData-TimingBias}).
%The testpulse is capacitively coupled into the circuit on the negative input $V_-$ of the comparator.
\paragraph{Circuit simulation}
Before the readout board was constructed, a simulation of the analog circuit was performed in LTspice\textsuperscript{\textregistered} \cite{LTSpice}. The main concern was the possibility of crosstalk between different \ac{SiPM} inputs, which is theoretically possible through the common $V_\text{com}$ rail. The employed capacitor on this rail seems to suppress the possibility of crosstalk in the simulation output. Therefore, the circuit was considered acceptable, and the readout board was built using this circuit.

\subsection{Signal sampling and time digitization}
\label{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-SamplingTDC}
\paragraph{Overview}
The first firmware block serves as a discriminator and \acf{TDC} for the \ac{SiPM} signals. It receives the comparator input signals $V_+$ and $V_-$ from the analog circuitry (sec.\ \ref{fig:Scint-ReadoutBoard-AnalogCircuit}) and determines whether there is a hit, depending on the voltage levels. Then, the hit is digitized with a \ac{TDC} running inside the \ac{FPGA} and made available to the next firmware block. The different sub-elements of the sampling logic and the working principle are illustrated in fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-SamplingTDC-Principle}. The logic block was also packaged as a fully documented, open-source \ac{IP core} which is available on the \ac{CERN} Git \cite{Multichannel-Time-Tagger-IP}.
\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Scint-Firmware-Sampling-Scheme.pdf}
    \caption{Logic components and working principle of the sampling block. The sampling of the input signal is performed with an \ac{FPGA}-internal comparator. The deserializer performs fast sampling of the comparator output for time-to-digital conversion. The rising and falling edges of the comparator signal are detected with dedicated logic.}
    \label{fig:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-SamplingTDC-Principle}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Signal discrimination}
The \ac{FPGA} is configured to use the internal comparators for analog input signal sampling (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard-FPGASampling}). The \texttt{IBUFDS} hardware primitives \cite{UltraScale-FPGA-SelectIO-Resources} of the \ac{FPGA} are instantiated for this purpose. The output of this primitive provides the comparator result determined by the input signals. As explained, the use of the \ac{FPGA}-internal comparators comes at the cost of the unknown bias voltage of each comparator, which is to be measured with a dedicated calibration procedure (sec.\ \ref{sec:ScintData-VoltageBias}).
\paragraph{Time digitization}
The comparator output signal is asynchronous, meaning its edges are not related to any clock, but are only determined by the changing input signals. The signal must be made synchronous (i.e.\ aligned with a clock) in order to perform further computations and transmit the data to the backend. This synchronization is performed by a \ac{TDC} (sec.\ \ref{sec:ReadoutElectronics-General}). While there exist discrete \ac{TDC} chips, in this case, the functionality is directly realized in \ac{FPGA} firmware.
\par To implement the \ac{TDC}, \texttt{ISERDESE3} deserializer hardware primitives \cite{UltraScale-FPGA-SelectIO-Resources} inside the \ac{FPGA} are used, which are originally designed to interface high-rate serial data transmissions. These components can operate at significantly higher clock frequencies than the \ac{FPGA} fabric itself \cite{UltraScale-FPGA-SelectIO-Resources}, and are provided with the system clock and a faster clock signal. With each system clock cycle, the deserializer outputs a bit vector of signals sampled at each fast clock edge. Of interest are only these cases where the comparator output value switches between \texttt{0} and \texttt{1}. In order not to transmit all data but only these relevant cases, a dedicated logic was implemented which scans the deserializer bit vector for \texttt{..01..} and \texttt{..10..} signatures. With a fast clock frequency of \SI{640}{\mega\hertz}, the implemented \ac{TDC} achieves a sampling granularity of \SI{0.78}{\nano\second}. This is the same granularity as in the \ac{OBDT} boards in the \ac{DT} system (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-Phase2Readout}).
\par The usage of the \ac{FPGA}-internal deserializers also imposes the need for a dedicated calibration procedure: There are non-deterministic delays between different units of different groups of \ac{FPGA} inputs, which means that the timing of the input channels must be calibrated after each startup of the device (sec.\ \ref{sec:ScintData-TimingBias}).

\subsection{Time-alignment unit}
\label{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-ProgrammableDelays}
The firmware offers the possibility to set individual delays for all input channels. This allows the application of the determined timing calibration for the channels (sec.\ \ref{sec:ScintData-TimingBias}) directly \enquote{online} in firmware, eliminating the need for timestamp realignment of the data in offline analysis.\footnote{This feature would be especially relevant if one would like to compute live coincidences or other timing-critical conditions, which is, however, not the case here.}
\paragraph{Programmable delay logic}
The programmable delay units for each input channel are realized with a data pipeline that has a flexible exit point. The hit data arrives at the pipeline input and is shifted one position forward with each clock cycle. The data output is connected to one of the pipeline positions. A user-writable register determines the position of the exit point of the pipeline to the output. With this setup, it is possible to select the channel delay with a precision equivalent to the system clock period, which in this case is \SI{6.25}{\nano\second}. The maximum delay that can be programmed is 32 clock cycles (\SI{200}{\nano\second}). Fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-DelayUnit-Scheme} illustrates the working principle of the described and implemented delay logic. An example for two initially misaligned input signals is presented in fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-DelayUnit-Timing}. It is visible that the signals become aligned by delaying both channels by the correct amount.

\subsection{Dead time unit}
\label{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-DeadTime}
To suppress potential afterpulses of a hit propagating through the readout, a dead time should be applied to each individual channel. These pulses may arise from \ac{SiPM} intrinsic afterpulses (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-SiPMs}) or from potential ringing of the analog circuit. Similar to the delay unit, it would be possible to apply such a dead time offline. However, it is advantageous to do it already in the \ac{FPGA}, to reduce the data rate transferred to the backend and consequently the required bandwidth of the data link to the backend.
\paragraph{Programmable dead time logic}
The dead time is set for each channel individually with a user-writable register. Each time a hit occurs, a counter is set to the dead time value specified in this register. With each clock cycle, the counter decreases its value until it reaches zero. A consecutive hit is only forwarded if the counter value is equal to zero. Note that with this simple logic, the achieved dead time is \enquote{extendable}, meaning it is restarted with each newly arriving hit. The granularity of the selectable dead time is again \SI{6.25}{\nano\second}. Fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-DeadTimeUnit-Timing} illustrates the working principle of the dead time unit with a timing diagram.

    \caption{Working principle of the implemented dead time unit illustrated with a timing diagram. After each arriving hit, the internal counter value is set to the programmed dead time (in the illustrated case two clock cycles) and then counts down. \subsection{Global timestamp assignment}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Hit arrival time translated to (absolute) timestamp
%    \item Same format as ODBT hits
%\end{itemize}
\label{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-TimestampAssignment}
Up to this point in the firmware, the hits are pipelined through the logic, but are not yet associated with a global timestamp. The transmission to the backend implies non-deterministic latencies of the hits, e.g.\ because of the buffering and funneling logic which is performed later in the firmware (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-Funneling}) and in the backend itself. As soon as these non-deterministic latencies appear, the hit timing information would be lost when it is not associated with a timestamp. The firmware block described in this section is responsible for assigning such a global timestamp to the hits in order to keep the hit timing information under the described circumstances.
\paragraph{Timestamp format}
The chosen timestamp format is the same as the timestamp format of the \acp{OBDT} in the \ac{DT} system (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-Phase2Readout}). This similarity simplifies the integration of the scintillator readout into the \ac{DT} readout chain. As introduced in sec.\ \ref{sec:CoordinateFrame-TimestampFormat}, there are three timing variables $TDC$ (TDC value), $BX$ (bunch crossing number), and $OC$ (orbit number). Only the first two are transmitted by the readout board, while the orbit number is added in the backend firmware. The width of one hit word with assigned timestamp is \SI{32}{\bit}, the bit mapping is presented in tab.\ \ref{tab:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-Timestamp-Bits}.
\begin{table}[htb]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|c||c|c|c|c|c|c|} 
        \hline
        Bits\texttt{[31:0]} & \texttt{31:28} & \texttt{27} & \texttt{26:25} & \texttt{24:20} & \texttt{19:8} & \texttt{7:0} \\ \hline
        Information & $-$ & Validity bit & $-$ & $TDC$ value & $BX$ value & Input channel no.\ \\ \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Bit mapping of one \SI{32}{\bit} hit word. Only when the validity bit equals \texttt{1}, a hit was detected for the given input channel.}
    \label{tab:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-Timestamp-Bits}
\end{table}
\paragraph{Synchronization with backend}
The clock of the readout system can be assumed to be synchronous with the \ac{DT} backend system, because the \ac{lpGBT} chip is used to recover the clock from the backend. More details on this are discussed in sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-lpGBTMezzanine}.
\par However, not only the clock, but also the timestamp values should be the same for all readout devices connected to the backend. To achieve this, one needs to receive a synchronization signal from the backend. The \ac{DT} backend sends a $BX$ reset signal (\texttt{BC0} signal) via the \ac{lpGBT} downlink to the readout board, which is received by the \ac{FPGA} firmware.\footnote{In \ac{CMS}, this \texttt{BC0} reset signal comes from the timing control system and is coupled to the clock of the \ac{LHC} accelerator. In the case of the test stand used in this work, it is simply emulated by the \ac{DT} backend system.} A dedicated logic block (the TTC receiver) was developed, which reconstructs a timestamp that is aligned with this reset signal. This aligned timestamp is then used when timing information is assigned to the recorded scintillator hits. In this way, it is ensured that the timestamps assigned to all events recorded by the \ac{DT} readout (\ac{OBDT} boards) and the scintillator readout board are aligned in time and \enquote{count up} synchronously.

\subsection{Hit buffer and funnel}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Limited bandwidth: Can not transmit hits of all channels together
%    \item However do not want to lose hits
%    \item Solution: Introduce individual buffer (FIFO) for all channels
%    \item Dynamically cycle through and activate outputs of different channel buffers
%    \item Reduces possible hit rate compared to no funnel
%\end{itemize}
\label{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-Funneling}
The bandwidth to transmit hit data from the readout board to the backend is limited. Therefore, one cannot send the hit information of all 32 channels at the same time. It is required to develop logic that funnels the parallel hit data of all channels to sequential transmission of hits. In order not to lose any hits when they can not all be sent in real time, they must be temporarily buffered. This hit buffering introduces a non-deterministic latency (mentioned already above in sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-TimestampAssignment}): The time it takes to transmit a hit depends on the current filling state of the buffers, which itself depends on the number of hits that have arrived before the current one.
\par While the buffering and sequential transmission can mitigate hit loss for a few occurring simultaneous hits, it is clear that as soon as the buffers are filled, hits will start to get lost. Since this thesis deals with a cosmic muon detector and not with extremely high data rates, one can accept this limitation.
\par The working principle of the developed buffering and funneling logic is illustrated in fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-Funneling-Scheme} and discussed in the following paragraphs.
\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{Scint-Firmware-BufferFunnel-Scheme.pdf}
    \caption{Working principle of the buffer and funnel logic in the firmware.}
    \label{fig:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-Funneling-Scheme}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Separate hit buffering}
For all 32 input channels, an individual hit buffer is employed in the firmware. This buffer is implemented as a so-called \ac{FIFO} unit. As the name suggests, this unit stores the incoming data and outputs them in the order of arrival. The depth of the used \ac{FIFO} is 128 hits for each channel. The \ac{FIFO} logic employs the \texttt{XPM\_FIFO\_ASYNC} macro, which is part of the \ac{XPM} library provided by the \ac{FPGA} vendor AMD\textsuperscript{\texttrademark} Xilinx \cite{Xilinx-XPM}.
\paragraph{Intelligent data funneling}
The desired funneling ratio is $32:1$, meaning that only one hit should be transmitted at a time. The funnel logic is realized with a demultiplexer, which selects one of the 32 \ac{FIFO} outputs and connects them to the next firmware block, the data transmission unit (fig.\ \ref{fig:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-Overview}).
\par There are different approaches to select the active \ac{FIFO} for transmission and cycle through the input channels. The easiest approach is to use a counter for the \ac{FIFO} selector to create a \enquote{rotating funnel}, giving all channels equal weight during hit transmission. However, if some channels experience higher rates than others, or only a few channels are used at all, this \ac{FIFO} selection is inefficient. Potentially empty \acp{FIFO} would still take away transmission time, which leads to less optimal usage of the available transmission bandwidth.
\par The funneling logic implemented in the readout firmware does not use this simple approach. It rather selects only the \acp{FIFO} that have at least one hit stored in them. The result is improved rate capability and therefore better performance, at the cost of increased complexity and therefore more used logic elements on the \ac{FPGA}.

\section{lpGBT mezzanine board}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Hardware interface between scintillator readout system and DT backend
%    \item OBDTs use lpGBT as well therefore compatible format
%    \item Synchronous clock (with backend) supplied by lpGBT chip
%\end{itemize\
\label{sec:Scint-lpGBTMezzanine}
As already mentioned above (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-Setup}), a second board was developed for the readout system, the \ac{lpGBT} mezzanine \ac{PCB}, which is built around the \ac{lpGBT} \ac{ASIC}. Its purpose is to create an interface from the \ac{FPGA} on the readout board to the \ac{DT} backend. The \ac{lpGBT} mezzanine board transmits the data via an optical fiber to the backend in a compatible format. The following sections should describe the \ac{ASIC} itself as well as the developed mezzanine board.

\subsection{lpGBT ASIC}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Low-power GigaBit Transceiver ASIC developed centrally by CERN
%    \item Key facts: Data transfer rate, clock recovery
%    \item Optical fiber interface to backend
%    \item Electrical link (eLink) interface to frontend FPGA
%    \item Control interface via optical fiber or wired I2C
%\end{itemize}
\label{sec:Scint-lpGBTMezzanine-lpGBT}
The \acf{lpGBT} \cite{lpGBT-Main} is an \ac{ASIC} developed by the \ac{CERN} electronics department for bidirectional data transfer via optical links in radiation environments. The chip is extensively used in the Phase-2 electronics of the \ac{CMS} detector, including the \ac{OBDT} boards of the \ac{DT} readout system (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-Phase2Readout}). This is the reason why the chip is also used in the self-developed readout electronics for the scintillator: The interface to the \ac{DT} backend can be realized on the basis of the existing hardware and software infrastructure. The central features of the chip are described in this section.
\paragraph{Optical links}
The \ac{lpGBT} \ac{ASIC} relies on two optical links: The \SI{2.56}{\giga\bit/\second} downlink, which receives data from the backend, and the \SI{5.12}{\giga\bit/\second} uplink, which transmits data to the backend. The data frames are received and transmitted with a frequency of \SI{40}{\mega\hertz}, which corresponds to the \ac{LHC} bunch crossing frequency (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-LHC}). The available data width of one uplink (downlink) frame is \SI{96}{\bit} (\SI{32}{\bit}). Depending on the operation mode of the chip, different data rates and frame widths are possible \cite{lpGBT-Main}.
\paragraph{Backend clock recovery}
The \ac{lpGBT} chip has the capability to recover the \SI{40}{\mega\hertz} clock of the backend directly from the optical downlink data \cite{lpGBT-Main}. This capability is a key feature, as it allows the operation of multiple independent electronic readout components in synchronization with each other. For the developed scintillator readout system, it ensures that the clock is in fact synchronous with the \ac{DT} backend and the \acp{OBDT} in the \ac{DT} minicrate, which is desired for simultaneous readout of the \ac{DT} chamber and the scintillator \cite{lpGBT-Main}.
% The electric-to-optical conversion of the data is not handled by the chip itself; one needs to connect it to an optical transmitter and receiver device.
\paragraph{eLink connections}
The connection of the chip to the readout electronics of the detector is implemented with regular electrical connections. The chip employs specific differential digital pairs to transfer data, which are called eLinks. In the uplink (downlink) direction, there are seven (four) groups containing four eLinks each. Each eLink receives or transmits a slice of the full data sequentially with a multiple of the \SI{40}{\mega\hertz} data frame frequency. The number of active eLinks and their frequency can be configured by the user \cite{lpGBT-Main}.
\par Apart from eLinks, the \ac{lpGBT} also features programmable differential clock outputs called eClocks. These output clocks with a programmable frequency operate in synchronization with the recovered \SI{40}{\mega\hertz} clock from the backend \cite{lpGBT-Main}.
