\section{The Standard Model of particle physics}
\label{sec:SM-Model}

The \ac{SM} is the state-of-the-art theory model used in particle physics, describing elementary particles and their interactions \cite{SM-Gaillard,SM-Weinberg,Book-Thomson}. It successfully predicts the results of many particle physics experiments to a very precise level at the reachable energy scales \cite{SM-CMSMeasurements}. The following section should provide a brief overview of the particles and fundamental forces in the \ac{SM}. The predicted particles in the \ac{SM} are shown in fig.\ \ref{fig:SM-Particles}. Despite its success, the model is considered incomplete \cite{Book-Thomson}, which will be mentioned briefly as well.

\subsection{Fermions}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Fermi-Dirac statistics \cite{FermiDirac-Dirac} \cite{FermiDirac-Fermi}the elementary particles
%\cite{Book-Thomson}
%    \item Pauli principle \cite{PauliPrinciple-Pauli}
%    \item 3 generations of matter
%    \item Quarks
%    \item Leptons
%\end{itemize}
All matter particles in the \ac{SM} are fermions which have half-integer spin and obey Fermi-Dirac statistics \cite{FermiDirac-Dirac,FermiDirac-Fermi}. The species of a matter particle is referred to as flavor. The \ac{SM} predicts three generations of spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ fermions, with four particles in each generation. While the particle masses increase with increasing fermion generation, the other properties of the respective particles from different generations are mostly similar. The first generation fermions form stable matter, i.e. atoms consisting of protons, neutrons\footnote{Protons $p(uud)$ and neutrons $n(udd)$ are composite particles of first generation quarks \cite{Book-Thomson}. Note that strictly, the free neutron is not a stable particle, however when bound a nucleus it does not decay \cite{PDG2024}.} and electrons, while higher generation fermions subsequently decay into lighter particles of the first generation. For all matter particles, there exists a corresponding antiparticle with similar properties except inverted charge, i.e.\ inverted sign of quantum numbers. Antiparticles are denoted with a bar over the particle symbol or their electric charge sign (with index $^\pm$) \cite{Book-Thomson,SM-Gaillard,Book-Griffiths}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Quarks} Six of the fermions in the \ac{SM} are quarks, named up ($u$), down ($d$), charm ($c$), strange ($s$), top ($t$) and bottom ($b$). One groups these into up-type ($u$, $c$, $t$) and down-type quarks ($d$, $s$, $b$), which carry electric charges of $\frac{2}{3}e$ and $-\frac{1}{3}e$ respectively.\footnote{$e=\SI{1.602e-19}{\coulomb}$ is the elementary charge, i.e. the absolute value of the electron charge \cite{PDG2024}.} Each fermion generation features one up- and one down-type particle. Quarks also carry one of three possible color charges (red, green, blue) which enables them to participate in strong interactions \cite{Book-Thomson,Book-Griffiths}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Leptons} The two other fermions in each generation are called leptons. One of these has an electric charge of $-1e$. The three charged leptons are the electron ($e$), the muon ($\mu$) and the tau ($\tau$). The other lepton in each generation is the electrically neutral and called neutrino, denoted as $\nu_e$, $\nu_\mu$ and $\nu_\tau$. In the \ac{SM}, neutrinos are considered massless \cite{Book-Thomson,Book-Griffiths}.

\subsection{Bosons}
%\begin{itemize}

%    \item Electroweak unification
%    \item Chromodynamics and color confinement
%\end{itemize}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Massive gauge bosons and fermions through spontaneous electroweak symmetry breaking \cite{SymmetryBreaking-Higgs} \cite{SymmetryBreaking-Englert}
%    \item Prediction of additional scalar boson

There are three fundamental interactions in the \ac{SM}: Electromagnetic interaction, weak interaction and strong interaction. These fundamental interactions are mediated by integer spin gauge bosons which obey Bose-Einstein statistics \cite{BoseEinstein-Bose}. In fact, all four mediator particles in the \ac{SM} have spin-$1$. Apart from the mediator bosons, there exists an additional spin-$0$ boson which arises from the Higgs mechanism \cite{Book-Thomson}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Photon ($\gamma$)}
The electromagnetic interaction is mediated by the photon ($\gamma$) and affects all particles which carry electric charge. The photon itself is massless and electrically neutral, giving the electromagnetic interaction an infinite range. The theory describing the electromagnetic interactions is called \ac{QED} \cite{Book-Griffiths}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{$W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons}
The weak interaction is mediated by the massive $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons and has a short range due to the large masses of the mediator particles. All fermions of the \ac{SM} can participate in this type of interaction. It is the only interaction to allow flavor changes of particles. Nuclear decay processes can be explained with this interaction. Electromagnetic and weak interaction were unified into the electroweak theory, which describes both effects as different manifestations of an electroweak interaction \cite{Book-Griffiths}. 
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Gluon ($g$)}
The strong interaction is mediated by the massless gluon ($g$). All particles carrying color change engage in strong interactions, which are the quarks and the gluon itself. While this interaction is the strongest one, it is also restricted in range due an effect referred to as color confinement. This effect arises from the self-coupling of the gluon and implies that one expects only color neutral bound states and no free quarks in nature \cite{Book-Thomson,SM-Gaillard}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Higgs boson ($H$)}
In the electroweak theory, the force mediator bosons are initially massless. However, the weak mediators are massive which was both experimentally measured but also required in order for weak interactions to be limited in range. The Higgs mechanism \cite{SymmetryBreaking-Higgs,SymmetryBreaking-Englert} resolves this issue employing spontaneous symmetry breaking. The mechanism leads to massive $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons and a massless photon while predicting the existence of an additional spin-$0$ boson, the Higgs ($H$) boson \cite{Book-Thomson}. This particle was ultimately discovered in 2012 at \acs{CERN} \cite{HiggsDiscovery-CMS,HiggsDiscovery-ATLAS}.

\subsection{Incompleteness and challenges}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Successful theory even in precision measurements
%    \item Missing quantum gravitation
%    \item Dark matter not explained \cite{DM-Trimble}
%    \item CP violation not allowed \cite{CPV-Sakharov}
%    \item Neutrino oscillations indicate massive neutrino(s), but massless in SM \cite{NO-Davis}
%\end{itemize}
While all particles predicted by the \ac{SM} have now been observed \cite{SM-Gaillard} and the model succeeds to predict their interaction properties to very high precision \cite{SM-CMSMeasurements}, the model is considered deficient. The incomplete list given here should point out the motivation future developments in particle physics theory and experiments.
\newline Firstly, the fourth fundamental interaction observed in nature, gravitation, is not included in the model. While gravitative interaction is significantly weaker than the three interactions of the \ac{SM} and therefore not relevant for high-energy physics experiments, the model remains incomplete \cite{SM-Gaillard}. Secondly, the observation of neutrino oscillations \cite{NO-Davis,Book-Thomson} implies non-zero neutrino masses, however in the \ac{SM} the neutrinos are considered massless.\footnote{Strictly speaking, not all neutrinos are required to be massive, since the oscillations only imply non-zero mass differences between the neutrino flavors.} Lastly, cosmic observations hint the existence of dark matter and dark energy, making up $\approx 95\%$ of the matter-energy density of the universe \cite{DM-Trimble, Book-Thomson}. These postulated constituents of the universe are not described by the \ac{SM}, it only attempts to model the remaining $\approx 5\%$ of baryonic matter. Additional shortcomings of the \ac{SM} are the unsatisfactory large number of parameters (26 parameters chosen from experimental observations) and the unresolved matter-antimatter asymmetry observed in nature \cite{Book-Thomson}.

\section{Properties of the muon}
\label{sec:SM-Muon}
This thesis focuses on the development of a readout system for a muon detector. Therefore, the properties of the particle of interest should be presented in this section in short.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Basic properties} The muon ($\mu$) is the second generation charged lepton. It has a mass of $m_\mu=\SI{105.658}{\mega\electronvolt/c^2}$ and is therefore $\approx 207\times$ heavier than its first generation relative, the electron ($e$). Since the muon is lighter than the lightest existing hadron (the $\pi^0$ meson), it can only decay leptonically \cite{PDG2024}\footnote{Decays with an associated photon or an additional electron-position pair ($e^+e^-$) are possible but strongly suppressed with branching fractions $\leq\mathcal{O}(10^{-5})$. Therefore, the shown decay mode is by far the dominating one \cite{PDG2024}.}:
\[
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        \mu^- &\rightarrow e^- + \bar{\nu_e} + \nu_\mu,
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        \mu^+ &\rightarrow e^+ + \nu_e + \bar{\nu_\mu}.
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\]
The muon lifetime is around $\tau_\mu=\SI{2.196}{\micro\second}$ \cite{PDG2024}. Due to time dilation, high-energy muons can travel significant distances (e.g. in Earth's atmosphere, as discussed in sec.\ \ref{sec:AtmosphericMuons}).
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{History of discovery} The muon was initially discovered in cloud chamber experiments with cosmic rays in 1937, where a charged particle more massive than the electron other than the proton was postulated \cite{MuonDiscovery-Anderson,MuonDiscovery-Street}. However, it was initially labeled "$\mu$-meson" and confused with the charged pions ($\pi^\pm$ mesons). The leptonic nature of the muon and the distinction from the pions was only revealed later, when a decay of pions into muons could be observed \cite{MuonDiscovery-Lee}.
%These mesons have masses in the same order of magnitude as the muon and were thought to be the mediator particle in Yukawa's theory of strong interactions \cite{MuonDiscovery-Yukawa}.

\subsection{Cosmic rays and muon creation}
\label{sec:AtmosphericMuons-Cosmics}

%    \item Origin of cosmic radiation \cite{Cosmics-Lingenfelter}
%    \item First observation of cosmic rays \cite{Cosmics-Hess}
%    \item Cosmic ray energy spectrum and particle types \cite{Cosmics-Grupen}
%\end{itemize}
%\begin{itemize}

Cosmic rays are particles reaching the Earth from space. The first indication of cosmic radiation was through balloon flight experiments of Hess in 1912, who discovered an increasing intensity of ionizing radiation with altitude \cite{Cosmics-Hess,Cosmics-Grupen}. Cosmic rays mainly consist of protons (Hydrogen nuclei), $\alpha$ particles (Helium nuclei) and heavier nuclei of a wide range of energies \cite{PDG2022,Cosmics-Gaisser,Book-Grieder}. These particles can have many origins, ranging our solar system to our galaxy to extragalactic sources. The understanding of cosmic ray sources is still an active field of research today \cite{Cosmics-Gaisser,Cosmics-Bindi}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Cosmic ray spectrum}
The flux of cosmic rays is highest for particle energies $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\giga\electronvolt})$, but the tail towards high energies extends beyond $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{Z\electronvolt})$ \cite{PDG2024}. The energy spectrum of cosmic rays is observed to approximately follow a power law with an energy-dependent spectral index $\gamma\approx 2.5-3.3$ \cite{PDG2024}:
\begin{equation}
    I(E)\propto E^{-\gamma}.
    \label{eq:Cosmics-Flux}
\end{equation}
The quantity $I=dN/(dE\; d\Omega\; dS\; dt)$ is the differential flux, i.e. the expected number $N$ of particles in the energy interval $dE$, the solid angle interval $d\Omega$, the area $dS$ and the time interval $dt$.

\paragraph{Interactions with Earth's atmosphere}
Interactions of the primary cosmic rays with Earth's atmosphere lead to energy loss of the cosmic particles and to a change in particle composition. In strong interactions, secondary particles can be produced. The most common secondary particles are pions ($\pi^\pm$, $\pi^0$). Other produced particles include kaons ($K^\pm$, $K^0$), hyperons and nucleons \cite{Book-Grieder}. Notably, high-energy cosmic rays can create large air showers in the atmosphere through cascades of interactions. This was first observed by Auger in 1939 through coincidences of particle detectors on the surface at far distances \cite{Cosmics-Auger}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Atmospheric muons}
Although their fraction in primary cosmic particles from space is negligible, muons have the largest fraction of charged particles arriving at Earth's surface \cite{PDG2022}. These muons originate from the decay of the secondary particles created in the atmosphere, mostly charged pions and kaons which have lifetimes of $\mathcal{O}(10^{-8}\;\si{\second})$ \cite{PDG2024}. This is too short for the particles to reach Earth's surface before they decay, even when accounting for time dilation. Charged pions decay to muons with a branching fraction of $\approx 0.999$ \cite{PDG2024}:\footnote{There are also suppressed decay modes with associated photons \cite{PDG2024}.}
\[
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        \pi^- &\rightarrow \mu^- + \bar{\nu_\mu},
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        \pi^+ &\rightarrow \mu^+ + \nu_\mu.
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\]
Similarly, charged kaons can decay to muons with a branching fraction of $\approx 0.635$ \cite{PDG2024}:\footnote{There are also semileptonic decay modes with associated pions with a branching fraction of $\approx 0.033$. As before, decays with associated photons are also possible but strongly suppressed \cite{PDG2024}.}
\[
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
         K^- &\rightarrow \mu^- + \bar{\nu_\mu},
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        K^+ &\rightarrow \mu^+ + \nu_\mu.
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\]
The decay of these particles is the main source for atmospheric muons. Most of the muons are produced at altitudes of $\approx 10-15\;\si{\kilo\meter}$ \cite{PDG2022,CosmicMuon-Shukla}.

\FloatBarrier
\subsection{Energy spectrum and angular distribution}
\label{sec:AtmosphericMuons-Dependencies}
In the following, the particle flux and its angular distribution of cosmic muons on Earth's surface should be briefly presented, as they are the baseline to calculate the expected detector response for the measurements presented in this thesis.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Vertical muon flux}
The flux of vertical atmospheric muons $\Phi(\theta=0)$ with energies above \SI{1}{\giga\electronvolt} at sea level can be measured as \cite{PDG2022,Book-Grieder}
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(E \geq \SI{1}{\giga\electronvolt},\;\theta=0) = \int_{\SI{1}{\giga\electronvolt}}^{\infty}dE\; I(E,\;\theta=0)\approx\SI{70}{\meter^{-2} \second^{-1} \steradian^{-1}}.
    \label{eq:CosmicMuon-VerticalFlux}
\end{equation}
The value depends not only on the altitude and location of the measurement, but also on the solar activity and atmospheric properties. %Also note that different measurements of absolute muon fluxes yield results spreading up to $\mathcal{O}(10\%)$, even though individual uncertainties are claimed to be smaller \cite{CosmicMuon-Cecchini}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Energy spectrum}
The mean energy of atmospheric muons is $\approx\SI{4}{\giga\electronvolt}$. While the muon energy spectrum is almost flat for energies of $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\giga\electronvolt})$, it becomes a similar power law dependence as the primary cosmic rays (eq.\ \ref{eq:Cosmics-Flux}) for the energies up to $\mathcal{O}(\SI{100}{\giga\electronvolt})$. Higher energies have even steeper energy spectra \cite{PDG2022}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Angular dependence}
The muon flux is both dependent on the azimuth ($\phi$) and zenith angle ($\theta$). The azimuthal dependence is small and will therefore be neglected in this work \cite{CosmicMuon-Shukla,Book-Grieder}. The zenith angle dependence is however significant. It arises mostly from the fact that different zenith angles imply different paths though the atmosphere, with varying length and atmospheric density \cite{Book-Grieder}. In the $\mathcal{O}(1-10\;\si{\giga\electronvolt})$ range, the dependence can be approximated with a cosine power law \cite{PDG2022,CosmicMuon-Cecchini}:
\begin{equation}
    I(E,\;\theta)\propto \cos^n\theta.
\end{equation}
The exponent $n$ has a value of $n\approx 2$ for $E\approx\SI{3}{\giga\electronvolt}$ and is slightly energy-dependent \cite{PDG2022}. For higher energies, the azimuthal dependence flattens, until it reaches an $I(E,\;\theta)\propto\text{sec}\,\theta$ relation for muon energies $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\tera\electronvolt})$ \cite{CosmicMuon-Cecchini}.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Prediction of detector rate}
The expected muon rate $\dot{N}$ for a detector can be obtained by integration of the differential flux $I$ over energy $E$, solid angle $\Omega$ and area $S$:
\begin{equation}
    \dot{N} = \int_{E_\text{thres}}^{\infty} dE \; \int d\Omega \; \int dS\; I(E,\;\theta) \cdot \varepsilon = \int d\Omega \; \int dS\; \Phi(E \geq E_\text{thres},\;\theta) \cdot \varepsilon. %\approx \SI{136.5}{\meter^{-2} \second^{-1}}
    \label{eq:CosmicMuon-DetectorRate}
\end{equation}
For this calculation, the energy detection threshold $E_\text{thres}$ of the detector must be taken into account. Note that the solution of the solid angle and area integrals is generally non-trivial for arbitrary detector geometries, since the effective detector area usually is a function of the solid angle. A detection efficiency $\varepsilon \leq 1$, which may even depend on the solid angle, must be considered as well.
\newline A very common approximation is the rate per area of a vertical detector as $\dot{N}/A \approx \SI{1}{\min^{-1}\centi\meter^{-2}}$ \cite{PDG2022}, which can be obtained by factorizing the solid angle and area integral in eq.\ \ref{eq:CosmicMuon-DetectorRate} and using the vertical flux from eq.\ \ref{eq:CosmicMuon-VerticalFlux}. Expectation values of cosmic muons will be calculated in different parts of this thesis, where they will be directly compared to the measurement.
