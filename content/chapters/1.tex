The \ac{SM} is the state-of-the-art theory model used in particle physics, describing elementary particles and their interactions \cite{SM-Gaillard,SM-Weinberg,Book-Thomson}. It successfully predicts the results of many particle physics experiments to a very precise level at the reachable energy scales \cite{SM-CMSMeasurements}. The following sections should provide a brief overview of the particles and fundamental forces in the \ac{SM}. The predicted particles in the \ac{SM} are shown in fig.\ \ref{fig:SM-Particles}. Despite its success, the current model is considered incomplete \cite{Book-Thomson}, which will be discussed briefly as well.

\subsection{Fermions}
%\begin{itemize}

All matter particles in the \ac{SM} are fermions which have half-integer spin and obey Fermi-Dirac statistics \cite{FermiDirac-Dirac,FermiDirac-Fermi}. The species of a matter particle is referred to as flavor. The \ac{SM} predicts three generations of spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ fermions, with four particles in each generation. Per generation there are two quarks (one up-type quark with electric charge $\frac{2}{3}e$ and one down-type quark with electric charge $-\frac{1}{3}e$) and two leptons (one charged lepton with electric charge $-1e$ and one electrically neutral and massless neutrino)\footnote{$e=\SI{1.602e-19}{\coulomb}$ is the elementary charge, i.e. the absolute value of the electron charge \cite{PDG2024}.}. While the particle masses increase with increasing fermion generation, other properties of the quarks and leptons from different generations are similar. The first generation fermions form stable matter, i.e. atoms consisting of protons, neutrons\footnote{Protons and neutrons are composite particles of first generation quarks. Note that the free neutron is not a stable particle, however when bound an a nucleus it does not decay.} and electrons, while higher generation fermions decay subsequently decay into lighter particles of the first generation. For all matter particles, there exists a corresponding antiparticle with similar properties except inverted charge (inverted sign of quantum numbers). Antiparticles are denoted with a bar over the particle symbol or their electric charge (with the index $^\pm$) \cite{Book-Thomson,SM-Gaillard}.

There are three fundamental interactions in the \ac{SM}: Electromagnetic interaction, weak interaction and strong interaction. These fundamental interactions are mediated by integer spin gauge bosons which obey Bose-Einstein statistics \cite{BoseEinstein-Bose}. In fact, all four mediator particles in the \ac{SM} have spin-$1$. The electromagnetic interaction is mediated by the photon ($\gamma$) and affects all particles which carry electric charge. The photon itself is massless and electrically neutral, giving the electromagnetic interaction an infinite range. The weak interaction is mediated by the massive $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons and has a short range due to the large masses of the mediator particles. It is the only interaction to allow flavor changes of particles. Electromagnetic and weak interaction were unified into the electroweak theory. The strong interaction is mediated by the massless gluon ($g$). All particles carrying color change engage in strong interactions, which are the quarks and the gluon itself. While this interaction is the strongest one, it is also restricted in range due an effect referred to as color confinement. This effect arises from the self-coupling of the gluon and implies that one expects only color neutral bound states and no free quarks in nature \cite{Book-Thomson,SM-Gaillard}.
\newline In the electroweak theory, the force mediator bosons are initially massless. However the weak mediators are massive which was both experimentally measured but also required in order for weak interactions to be limited in range. The proposed Higgs mechanism \cite{SymmetryBreaking-Higgs,SymmetryBreaking-Englert} resolves this issue emplyoing spontaneous symmetry breaking. The mechanism leads to massive $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons and a massless photon while predicting the existance of an additional spin-$0$ boson, the Higgs ($H$) boson \cite{Book-Thomson}. This particle was ultimately discovered in 2012 at CERN \cite{HiggsDiscovery-CMS,HiggsDiscovery-ATLAS}.

\subsection{Incompleteness and challenges}

%    \item Successful theory even in precision measurements
%    \item Missing quantum gravitation
%    \item Dark matter not explained \cite{DM-Trimble}
%    \item CP violation not allowed \cite{CPV-Sakharov}

While all particles predicted by the \ac{SM} have now been observed \cite{SM-Gaillard} and the model succeeds to predict their interaction properties to very high precision \cite{SM-CMSMeasurements}, the model is considered deficient. Firstly, the fourth fundamental interaction observed in nature, gravitation, is not included in the model. While gravitatative interaction is significantly weaker than the three interactions of the \ac{SM} and therefore not relevant for high-energy physics experiments, the model remains incomplete \cite{SM-Gaillard}. Secondly, the observation of neutrino oscillations \cite{NO-Davis,Book-Thomson} implies non-zero neutrino masses\footnote{Strictly speaking, not all neutrinos are required to be massive, since the oscillations only imply non-zero mass differences between the neutrino flavors.}, however in the \ac{SM} the neutrinos are considered massless. Cosmic observations hint the existance of dark matter and dark energy, making up $\approx 95\%$ of the matter-energy density of the universe \cite{DM-Trimble, Book-Thomson}. However the \ac{SM} only attempts to model the $\approx 5\%$ of baryonic matter. There are additional shortcomings of the \ac{SM} as the unsatisfactory large number of parameters (26 parameters chosen from experimental observations) and the unresolved matter-antimatter asymmetry observed in nature \cite{Book-Thomson}. This incomplete list should point out the motivation future developments in particle physics theory and experiments.



\section{Properties of the muon}
\label{sec:SM-Muon}
This thesis focuses on the development of a readout system for a muon detector. Therefore the properties of the particle of interest should be presented in this section in short.
\newline The muon ($\mu^-$) is the second generation charged fermion. It has an electric charge of $-1e$ and a mass of $m_\mu=\SI{105.658}{\mega\electronvolt/c^2}$. It is therefore $\approx 207\times$ heavier than its first generation relative, the electron ($e^-$). Since the muon is lighter than the lightest existing hadron (the $\pi^0$ meson), it can only decay leptonically \cite{PDG2024}:\footnote{Decays with an associated photon or an additional electron-position pair ($e^+e^-$) are possible but strongly suppressed with branching fractions $\leq\mathcal{O}(10^{-5}$). Therefore shown decay mode is by far the dominating one \cite{PDG2024}.}
\[
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        \mu^- &\rightarrow e^- + \bar{\nu_e} + \nu_\mu
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        \mu^+ &\rightarrow e^+ + \nu_e + \bar{\nu_\mu}
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\]
The muon lifetime is around $\tau_\mu=\SI{2.196}{\micro\second}$ \cite{PDG2024}. Due to time dilation, high-energy muons can travel significant distances (e.g. in Earth's atmosphere, as discussed in sec.\ \ref{sec:AtmosphericMuons}).
\newline The muon was initially discovered in cloud chamber experiments with cosmic rays in 1937, where a charged particle more massive than the electron other than the proton was postulated \cite{MuonDiscovery-Anderson,MuonDiscovery-Street}. However, it was initially labeled "$\mu$-meson" and confused with the charged pions ($\pi^\pm$ mesons). These mesons have masses in the same order of magnitudeas the muon and were thought to be the mediator particle in Yukawa's theory of strong interactions \cite{MuonDiscovery-Yukawa}. The leptonic nature of the muon and the distinction from the pions was only revealed later, when a decay of pions into muons could be observed \cite{MuonDiscovery-Lee}.

\subsection{Cosmic rays and muons}
\label{sec:AtmosphericMuons-Cosmics}

Cosmic rays are particles reaching the Earth from space. The first indication of cosmic ratiation was through balloon flight experiments of Hess in 1912, who discovered an increasing intensity of ionizing radiation with altitude \cite{Cosmics-Hess,Cosmics-Grupen}. Cosmic rays mainly consist of protons, alpha particles and heavier nuclei of a wide range of energies \cite{PDG2022,Cosmics-Gaisser,Book-Grieder}. These particles can have many different origins, ranging our solar system to our galaxy to extragalactic sources. The understanding of cosmic ray sources is still an active field of research today \cite{Cosmics-Gaisser,Cosmics-Bindi}. The flux of cosmic rays is highest for particle energies $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\giga\electronvolt})$ but the tail towards high energies extends beyond $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{Z\electronvolt})$ \cite{PDG2024}. The energy spectrum of cosmic rays is observed to approximately follow a power law with an energy-dependent spectral index $\gamma$ \cite{PDG2024}:
\begin{equation*}
    I(E)\propto E^{-\gamma},
    \quad\text{where } \gamma \approx 
    \left\{
        \begin{array}{@{}l  r @{} c @{} l}
            \; 2.7, & \mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\giga\electronvolt})\; & \leq E \leq & \;\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\peta\electronvolt}) \\
            \; 3.0, & \mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\peta\electronvolt})\; & \leq E \leq & \;\mathcal{O}(\SI{100}{\peta\electronvolt}) \\
            \; 3.3, & \mathcal{O}(\SI{100}{\peta\electronvolt})\; & \leq E \leq & \;\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\exa\electronvolt}) \\
            \; 2.5, & \mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\exa\electronvolt})\; & \leq E \\
        \end{array}
    \right.
\end{equation*}
\newline Interactions of the primary cosmic rays with Earth's atmosphere lead to energy loss of the cosmic particles and to a change in particle composition. In strong interactions, secondary particles can be prodced. The most common secondary particles are pions ($\pi^\pm$, $\pi^0$), however there are also many other particles produced, such as kaons ($K^\pm$, $K^0$), hyperons and nucleons \cite{Book-Grieder}. Notably, cosmic rays can create large air showers in the atmosphere through cascades of interactions. This was first observed by Auger in 1939 through concidences of particle detectors on the surface at far distances \cite{Cosmics-Auger}.
\newline Although their fraction in primary cosmic particles from space is neglible, muons have the largest fraction of charged particles arriving on Earth's surface \cite{PDG2022}. These muons originate from the decay of the secondary particles created in Earth's atmosphere. Charged pions and kaons have lifetimes of $\mathcal{O}(10^{-8}\;\si{\second})$ \cite{PDG2024} which is too short to reach Earth's surface, even when accounting for time dilation. The charged pions decay to muons with a branching fraction of $\approx 0.999$ \cite{PDG2024}:\footnote{There are also suppressed decay modes with associated photons \cite{PDG2024}.}
\[
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        \pi^- &\rightarrow \mu^- + \bar{\nu_\mu}
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        \pi^+ &\rightarrow \mu^+ + \nu_\mu
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\]
Similarly the charged kaons can decay to muons with a branching fraction of $\approx 0.635$ \cite{PDG2024}:\footnote{There are also semileptonic decay modes with associated pions with a branching fraction of $\approx 0.033$. As before, decays with associated photons are also possible but strongly suppressed \cite{PDG2024}.}
\[
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
         K^- &\rightarrow \mu^- + \bar{\nu_\mu}
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{align*}
        K^+ &\rightarrow \mu^+ + \nu_\mu
    \end{align*}
\end{minipage}
\]
Most muons are produced at altitudes of $\approx 10-15\;\si{\kilo\meter}$ and lose $\approx\SI{2}{\giga\electronvolt}$ before reaching the surface \cite{PDG2022,CosmicMuon-Shukla}.

\subsection{Energy spectrum and angular distribution}
\label{sec:AtmosphericMuons-Dependencies}

In the following, the energy spectrum and angular distribution of cosmic muons on Earth's surface should be breifly presented, as they are the baseline to calculate the expected detector response for the measurements presented in this thesis. In principle, calculations of these quantities is quite complex, due to the variety of decay processes and energy loss mechanisms that can occur. However there are useful parametrizations of the measured muon spectra which allow for a simplified model to be used for simulations or approximate predictions \cite{CosmicMuon-Shukla}.
\newline A good approximation of the flux of the vertical (zenith angle $\theta=0$) atmospheric muons is provided by the following formula \cite{CosmicMuon-Shukla}:
\begin{equation*}
    I(E,\;\theta=0) \propto (E_0+E)^{-n}\left(1+\frac{E}{\epsilon}\right)^{-1}
\end{equation*}
In this formula, $E_0$ is the energy loss of the muon from the point of its creation to the surface, and assumed constant. This is of course an approximation, especially for lower muon energies, as the energy loss is not really independent of the particle energy (see sec.\ \ref{sec:Interactions}). Before the meson decays into the muon, it also loses energy on its way towards the surface. When the energy of this particle (and consequently of the resulting muon) is higher, the meson can lose more energy before it decays. This effect is accounted for with the factor $\epsilon>1$ \cite{CosmicMuon-Shukla}.
\newline The dependence of the muon flux on the azimut angle $\phi$ is small and will therefore be neglected here. However there is a dependence on the zenith angle $\theta$ which can be parametrized as follows \cite{CosmicMuon-Shukla}:
\begin{equation*}
    I(E,\;\theta)\propto (E_0+E)^{-n}\left(1+\frac{E}{\epsilon}\right)^{-1}D(\theta)^{-(n-1)}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
    \text{where } D(\theta)=\sqrt{\left( \frac{R^2}{d^2}\cos^2\theta+\frac{2R}{d}+1 \right)}-\frac{R}{d}\cos\theta
\end{equation*}
This formula takes into account the variation in muon path length to the surface for different zenith angles assuming a spherical Earth. $R=\SI{6378}{\kilo\meter}$ is the radius at the Earth's surface \cite{PDG2024}\footnote{The given value is the nominal Earth equatorial radius and should serve as an approximation for the spherical Earth assumption of the formula.} and $d=\mathcal{O}(\SI{10}{\kilo\meter})$ is the vertical path length that the muon travels \cite{PDG2022,CosmicMuon-Shukla}. When approximating $R \gg d$ (which essentially translates to assuming a flat Earth), one obtains \cite{CosmicMuon-Shukla}
\begin{equation*}
    D(\theta) \approx 1/\cos\theta
\end{equation*}
This yields the more commonly known approximation of the atmospheric muon angular dependence with a cosine power law with typical values of $n\approx 3$ (also see sec.\ \ref{sec:AtmosphericMuons-Cosmics}) \cite{CosmicMuon-Shukla}:
\begin{equation}
    I(E,\;\theta)\propto (E_0+E)^{-n}\left(1+\frac{E}{\epsilon}\right)^{-1}\cos^{(n-1)}\theta
    \label{eq:AtmosphericMuon-Depedence}
\end{equation}

\newline The expected muon rate for a detector with area $A$ can now be obtained by integration over energy, solid angle and area
\begin{equation*}
    \dot{N} = \int_{E_\text{thres}}^{\infty}\text{d}E \; \int\text{d}\Omega \; \int\text{d}A\; I(E,\;\theta) %\approx \SI{136.5}{\meter^{-2} \second^{-1}}
\end{equation*}
