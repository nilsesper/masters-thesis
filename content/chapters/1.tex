The \ac{SM} is the state-of-the-art theory model used in particle physics, describing elementary particles and their interactions \cite{SM-Gaillard,SM-Weinberg,Book-Thomson}. It successfully predicts the results of many particle physics experiments to a very precise level at the reachable energy scales \cite{SM-CMSMeasurements}. The following sections should provide a brief overview of the particles and fundamental forces in the \ac{SM}. The predicted particles in the \ac{SM} are shown in fig.\ \ref{fig:SM-Particles}. Despite its success, the current model is considered incomplete \cite{Book-Thomson}, which will be discussed briefly as well.

\subsection{Fermions}

All matter particles in the \ac{SM} are fermions which have half-integer spin and obey Fermi-Dirac statistics \cite{FermiDirac-Dirac,FermiDirac-Fermi}. The species of a matter particle is referred to as flavor. The \ac{SM} predicts three generations of spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ fermions, with four particles in each generation. Per generation there are two quarks (one up-type quark with electric charge $\frac{2}{3}e$ and one down-type quark with electric charge $-\frac{1}{3}e$) and two leptons (one charged lepton with electric charge $-1e$ and one electrically neutral and massless neutrino)\footnote{$e$ is the elementary charge, i.e. the absolute value of the electron charge.}. While the particle masses increase with increasing fermion generation, other properties of the quarks and leptons from different generations are similar. The first generation fermions form stable matter, i.e. atoms consisting of protons, neutrons\footnote{Protons and neutrons are composite particles of first generation quarks. Note that the free neutron is not a stable particle, however when bound an a nucleus it does not decay.} and electrons, while higher generation fermions decay subsequently decay into lighter particles of the first generation. For all matter particles, there exists a corresponding antiparticle with similar properties except inverted charge (inverted sign of quantum numbers). Antiparticles are denoted with a bar over the particle symbol or their electric charge (with the index $^\pm$) \cite{Book-Thomson,SM-Gaillard}.

There are three fundamental interactions in the \ac{SM}: Electromagnetic interaction, weak interaction and strong interaction. These fundamental interactions are mediated by integer spin gauge bosons which obey Bose-Einstein statistics \cite{BoseEinstein-Bose}. In fact, all four mediator particles in the \ac{SM} have spin-$1$. The electromagnetic interaction is mediated by the photon ($\gamma$) and affects all particles which carry electric charge. The photon itself is massless and electrically neutral, giving the electromagnetic interaction an infinite range. The weak interaction is mediated by the massive $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons and has a short range due to the large masses of the mediator particles. It is the only interaction to allow flavor changes of particles. Electromagnetic and weak interaction were unified into the electroweak theory. The strong interaction is mediated by the massless gluon ($g$). All particles carrying color change engage in strong interactions, which are the quarks and the gluon itself. While this interaction is the strongest one, it is also restricted in range due an effect referred to as color confinement. This effect arises from the self-coupling of the gluon and implies that one expects only color neutral bound states and no free quarks in nature \cite{Book-Thomson,SM-Gaillard}.
\newline In the electroweak theory, the force mediator bosons are initially massless. However the weak mediators are massive which was both experimentally measured but also required in order for weak interactions to be limited in range. The proposed Higgs mechanism \cite{SymmetryBreaking-Higgs,SymmetryBreaking-Englert} resolves this issue emplyoing spontaneous symmetry breaking. The mechanism leads to massive $W^\pm$ and $Z^0$ bosons and a massless photon while predicting the existance of an additional spin-$0$ boson, the Higgs ($H$) boson \cite{Book-Thomson}. This particle was ultimately discovered in 2012 at CERN \cite{HiggsDiscovery-CMS,HiggsDiscovery-ATLAS}.

\subsection{Incompleteness and challenges}

%    \item Missing quantum gravitation
%    \item Dark matter not explained \cite{DM-Trimble}
%    \item CP violation not allowed \cite{CPV-Sakharov}

While all particles predicted by the \ac{SM} have now been observed \cite{SM-Gaillard} and the model succeeds to predict their interaction properties to very high precision \cite{SM-CMSMeasurements}, the model is considered deficient. Firstly, the fourth fundamental interaction observed in nature, gravitation, is not included in the model. While gravitatative interaction is significantly weaker than the three interactions of the \ac{SM} and therefore not relevant for high-energy physics experiments, the model remains incomplete \cite{SM-Gaillard}. Secondly, the observation of neutrino oscillations \cite{NO-Davis,Book-Thomson} implies non-zero neutrino masses\footnote{Strictly speaking, not all neutrinos are required to be massive, since the oscillations only imply non-zero mass differences between the neutrino flavors.}, however in the \ac{SM} the neutrinos are considered massless. Cosmic observations hint the existance of dark matter and dark energy, making up $\approx 95\%$ of the matter-energy density of the universe \cite{DM-Trimble, Book-Thomson}. However the \ac{SM} only attempts to model the $\approx 5\%$ of baryonic matter. There are additional shortcomings of the \ac{SM} as the unsatisfactory large number of parameters (26 parameters chosen from experimental observations) and the unresolved matter-antimatter asymmetry observed in nature \cite{Book-Thomson}. This incomplete list should point out the motivation future developments in particle physics theory and experiments.



\section{Properties of the muon}
This thesis focuses on the development of a readout system for a muon detector. Therefore the properties of the particle of interest should be presented in this section in short.
\newline The muon ($\mu$) is the second generation charged fermion. It has a mass of $m_\mu=\SI{105.658}{\mega\electronvolt/c^2}$ and is therefore $\approx 207\times$ heavier than its first generation relative, the electron \cite{PDG2024}. Since the muon is lighter than the lightest existing hadron (the $\pi^0$ meson \cite{PDG2024}), it can only decay leptonically:
\[
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{equation*}
        \mu^- \rightarrow e^- + \bar{\nu_e} + \nu_\mu
    \end{equation*}
\end{minipage}
\begin{minipage}{0.5\linewidth}
    \begin{equation*}
        \mu^+ \rightarrow e^+ + \nu_e + \bar{\nu_\mu}
    \end{equation*}
\end{minipage}
\]
The muon lifetime is around $\tau_\mu=\SI{2.196}{\micro\second}$ \cite{PDG2024}. Due to time dilation, high-energy muons can travel significant distances (e.g. in Earth's atmosphere, as discussed in sec.\ \ref{sec:AtmosphericMuons}).
\newline The muon was initially discovered in cloud chamber experiments with cosmic rays in 1937, where a charged particle more massive than the electron other than the proton was postulated \cite{MuonDiscovery-Anderson,MuonDiscovery-Street}. However, it was initially labeled "$\mu$-meson" and confused with the charged pions ($\pi^\pm$ mesons). These mesons have masses in the same order of magnitudeas the muon and were thought to be the mediator particle in Yukawa's theory of strong interactions \cite{MuonDiscovery-Yukawa}. The leptonic nature of the muon and the distinction from the pions was only revealed later, when a decay of pions into muons could be observed \cite{MuonDiscovery-Lee}.
