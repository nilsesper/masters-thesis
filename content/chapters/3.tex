\paragraph{LHC accelerator}
The \ac{LHC} currently is the largest and most powerful particle accelerator in the world. It is a circular collider, creating proton-proton collisions with a \ac{COME} of up to $\sqrt{s}=\SI{14}{\tera\electronvolt}$. The accelerator has a circumference of \SI{27}{\kilo\meter} and was constructed in the tunnel originally used for the \ac{LEP} \cite{LEP-Main}, which lies around \SI{100}{\meter} below the surface. In order to guide the particles, the machine uses superconducting magnets with field strengths above \SI{8}{\tesla}. Particle acceleration is provided by superconducting cavities. Particle collisions take place with a bunch-crossing frequency of \SI{40}{\mega\hertz}. Originally, the \ac{LHC} was designed to provide an instantaneous luminosity of $\mathcal{L}=10^{34}\;\si{\centi\meter^{-2} \second^{-1}}$ at the interaction points \cite{LHC-Main}. In the context of the \ac{HLLHC} project, the accelerator will be upgraded to reach instantaneous luminosities of $\mathcal{L}=\num{5e34}\;\si{\centi\meter^{-2} \second^{-1}}$ \cite{HLLHC-TDR}. It should be noted that the \ac{LHC} is not only capable of accelerating protons but also heavy ions \cite{LHC-Main}.

Protons are extracted from a hydrogen bottle and first accelerated in a linear accelerator (LINAC 4) to \SI{160}{\mega\electronvolt} \cite{LINAC4-Main}. Then they are injected in a series of circular accelerators (Booster, Proton Synchrotron, Super Proton Synchrotron) and finally injected into the \ac{LHC} at an energy of \SI{450}{\giga\electronvolt} \cite{LHC-Main}.
\paragraph{LHC operation}
The accelerator operation can be divided into different run periods, each spanning several years. Run-1 from $2010$ to $2013$ was the initial operation period at lower \ac{COME} \cite{LHC-Run1}, which already led to the discovery of the Higgs boson \cite{HiggsDiscovery-CMS,HiggsDiscovery-ATLAS}. Run-2 lasted from $2015$ to $2018$ \cite{LHC-Run2}. Run-3 started in $2022$ and should last until the summer of $2026$. After this run, the \ac{HLLHC} upgrade program will be implemented in the \ac{LS3} \cite{LHC-Run3}.

\subsection{Experiment overview}
\label{sec:CMS-Overview}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item CMS rendering
%    \item Brief listing of all subdetectors
%\end{itemize}
The \ac{CMS} experiment \cite{CMS-Main} is one of two multipurpose particle detectors operated at the \ac{LHC}. It is located approximately \SI{100}{\meter} underground close to the French village Cessy. It consists of a cylindrical barrel with five wheels and two disk-shaped endcaps. The detector measures \SI{21.6}{\meter} in length and \SI{14.6}{\meter} in diameter, which is significantly more compact than the second multipurpose detector at the \ac{LHC}, named \ac{ATLAS} \cite{ATLAS-Main}. Apart from the compactness, the detector was designed to provide good performance especially in the detection of muons. Both of these aspects are represented in the name of the experiment. The \ac{CMS} assembly has a total mass of around \SI{12500}{\tonne} \cite{CMS-Main}. Fig.\ \ref{fig:CMS-RenderingRun2} shows a rendering of the \ac{CMS} detector in its configuration after Run-2.

    \label{fig:CMS-RenderingRun2}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Phase-2 upgrade}
With the \ac{HLLHC} project (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-LHC}), the luminosity of the accelerator will be significantly increased. This implies significantly stronger radiation doses, especially to the inner and forward subdetectors. Also, the pileup, i.e.\ the number of collisions within one bunch crossing, will increase up to $200$, imposing a challenge to the detectors. The readout systems and trigger algorithms for all subdetectors need to be revised to maintain acceptable physics performance under these conditions. In addition, the longevity and radiation hardness of the components has to be evaluated \cite{CMS-Phase2}.
\paragraph{Coordinate frame}
Before the subdetectors are described, the coordinate frame used in \ac{CMS} should be introduced. The center of the frame is the nominal interaction point, where the two proton beams are expected to collide. The $x$-axis points towards the \ac{LHC} center, and the $y$-axis points upwards. The $z$-axis points along the beam line. Frequently, spherical coordinates $(r,\;\theta,\;\phi)$ are used, with the azimuthal angle $\phi$ in the $x$-$y$-plane and the zenith angle $\theta$ measured from the $z$-axis \cite{CMS-Main}. Instead of the zenith angle, the so-called pseudorapidity $\eta$ is frequently used, which relates to the angle as follows \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}:
\begin{equation}
    \eta=-\ln\tan\frac{\theta}{2}.
\end{equation}
This definition is convenient since in the high-energy limit $E_z \gg M$ the Lorentz-invariant rapidity $y=\artanh(v_z/c)$ of a particle can be approximated with the pseudorapidity $\eta$ \cite{Book-KolanoskiWermes}.

\subsection{Subdetectors}
\label{sec:CMS-Subdetectors}
In the following paragraphs, the subdetectors of \ac{CMS} should be briefly described. It should be noted that the detector has already been upgraded multiple times and parts were exchanged. Since this work is in the context of the Phase-2 upgrade (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-Overview}), the subdetectors should be described in their foreseen state after this upgrade.
\paragraph{Tracker}
The particle tracking system is the innermost subdetector of \ac{CMS}. Charged particles leave hits in multiple layers of this subdetector, which allows the reconstruction of tracks. The system can be divided in the \ac{IT} and the \ac{OT}. The \ac{IT} is composed of silicon pixel detectors with segmentation in the order of $50\times 50\;\si{\micro\meter^2}$. It has around $2$ billion individual channels \cite{CMS-Phase2Tracker-ConferenceReport}. The \ac{OT} uses both silicon strip and pixel-strip modules and has a total of $213$ million readout channels \cite{CMS-Phase2Tracker-ConferenceReport}. With the Phase-2 tracker, information from the subdetector is introduced in the \ac{CMS} trigger for the first time. This is done measuring particle hits in two adjacent silicon sensors and determining the particle transverse momentum ($p_T$) by the track curvature. The acceptance of the tracking system is $\left| \eta \right|\leq 4$ \cite{CMS-Phase2Tracker}.
\paragraph{Electromagnetic calorimeter}
The \ac{ECAL} measures the energy of particles which create electromagnetic showers, namely electrons and photons. In the barrel region it consists of \num{61200} lead tungstate (PbWO$_4$) scintillator crystals which are read out by \acp{APD} \cite{CMS-Main}. In the endcaps, the current calorimeter will be replaced with the so-called \ac{HGCAL}. This calorimeter consists of silicon modules as well plastic scintillators read out by \acp{SiPM}. The \ac{HGCAL} will be able to resolve the particle showers along the propagation axis. The high granularity is reflected in the number of channels, which is about $6.1$ million. The \ac{ECAL} can measure particles in the angular range $\left| \eta \right|\leq 3$ \cite{CMS-Phase2BarrelCalo,CMS-Phase2EndcapCalo,CMS-Phase2}.
\paragraph{Hadronic calorimeter}
Particles which produce hadronic showers are detected in the \ac{HCAL}. The barrel region consists of a sampling calorimeter made from \num{70000} scintillator tiles and brass absorbers, read out by \acp{SiPM} \cite{CMS-Main}. The existing endcap calorimeter will be replaced as well by the \ac{HGCAL}, which not only features electromagnetic but also hadronic calometry capabilities \cite{CMS-Phase2BarrelCalo,CMS-Phase2EndcapCalo}.
\paragraph{Superconducting solenoid}
The magnetic field in the detector is generated by a large \SI{6}{\meter}-diameter superconducting solenoid, which is located outside the tracker and calorimeters.\footnote{Strictly speaking, the \ac{HO} is located outside of the solenoid, but in this coarse overview of the \ac{CMS} detector this is not relevant \cite{CMS-Main}.} Approximately \SI{20}{\kilo\ampere} of current flowing through the chilled NbTi superconductor generate a central magnetic field of \SI{3.8}{\tesla}. Through the bending of charged particle tracks in the field, the particle momentum and charge can be determined. The magnetic flux is returned through a \SI{10000}{\tonne} iron yoke assembly which comprises both the outer barrel and endcap region \cite{CMS-Main}.
\paragraph{Muon detectors}
The \ac{CMS} detector in its Phase-2 configuration will consist of four types of muon detectors, all of which are gaseous detectors. In the barrel region (angular range $\left| \eta \right|\leq 1.2$), four layers of \ac{DT} chambers perform muon detection and tracking. The \ac{DT} system will be described in detail in the following section, as it is relevant for this work (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-DT}).
\newline In the endcap region, \acp{CSC} are used which cover a pseudorapidity of $0.9\leq \left| \eta \right|\leq 2.4$. Both the barrel and endcap region also feature \ac{RPC} detectors to provide redundancy. In the context of the Phase-2 upgrade, \ac{GEM} detectors are introduced to \ac{CMS}, namely the ME0 station which will extend the overall muon acceptance to $\left| \eta \right|\leq 2.8$ \cite{CMS-Main,CMS-Phase2Muon}. The GE1/1 \ac{GEM} station was already installed in \ac{CMS} before the Phase-2 upgrade activities start in \ac{LS3} \cite{PhD-Mocellin}. The muon system has a total detector area of \SI{25000}{\meter^2}, making it by far the largest subdetector in \ac{CMS} \cite{CMS-Main,CMS-Phase2Muon}.
\paragraph{Data acquisition and trigger}
The bunch crossing frequency of the \ac{LHC} of \SI{40}{\mega\hertz} is orders of magnitude higher than the rate of events that can be stored. Therefore, rate reduction of events is inevitable. \ac{CMS} implements a two-stage trigger system, consisting of the \ac{L1T} and the \ac{HLT}. With the Phase-2 upgrade, the \ac{L1T} acceptance rate is significantly increased from \SI{100}{\kilo\hertz} to \SI{750}{\kilo\hertz}, increasing the input data rate to approximately \SI{63}{\tera\bit/\second}. The \ac{L1T} logic is running on \acp{FPGA} to achieve the desired performance. Depending on the \ac{L1T} accept signal, the readout of the full detector is executed, and the data is further evaluated by the \ac{HLT}. \ac{HLT} algorithms are executed mostly on microprocessors in a computing farm. The final rate of events to be stored by the \ac{DAQ} and analyzed offline is estimated to be up to \SI{7.5}{\kilo\hertz} \cite{CMS-Main,CMS-Phase2L1Trigger,CMS-Phase2DAQHLT}.

In total, the \ac{DT} system has \num{171852} readout channels \cite{CMS-Main,CMS-Muon}. \paragraph{Chamber setup}
A drift chamber measures around $2\times 2.5 \;\si{\meter^2}$ in area, with the outer chambers being slightly larger. Most chambers consist of three so-called \acfp{SL}. These superlayers are themselves constructed from four layers of drift cells, which are staggered by half the cell width for adjacent layers. The drift cell wires of \acp{SL} 1 and 3 are oriented along the beam axis, giving sensitivity along the $r$-$\phi$-direction. The \ac{SL} 2 is rotated by $90^\circ$, giving sensitivity along the $z$-$\theta$-direction.\footnote{The MB4 chambers only have two \acp{SL}, which are both oriented along the $r$-$\phi$-direction \cite{CMS-Main}.} The number of channels per chamber lies in $\mathcal{O}(700)$ and varies with the chamber size \cite{CMS-Main,CMS-Muon,CMS-Phase2Muon}. Fig.\ \ref{fig:DT-Chamber} shows an exposed view of one \ac{DT} chamber, highlighting its structure.
\paragraph{Drift cell}
A close-up view of one drift cell is presented in fig.\ \ref{fig:DT-Cell}. It measures $42\times 13\;\si{\milli\meter^2}$ along its cross section. The cell length is determined by the chamber dimensions. The cell walls are the so-called I-beams, which are aluminum structures. These structures not only hold the cathode electrodes, but also provide structural integrity to the chamber. In the center of the cell is the anode wire, which is made from gold-plated steel with a diameter of \SI{50}{\micro\meter}. Two additional electrodes are located on the walls above and below the wire, to shape the electric field. The gas mixture used in the \ac{DT} system is composed of 85\% Argon and 15\% CO$_2$ (volume percentage). The voltages applied to the electrodes are as follows: $+3.6\;\si{\kilo\volt}$ to the wire, $-1.2\;\si{\kilo\volt}$ to the cathode strips and $+1.8\;\si{\kilo\volt}$ to the field-shaping electrodes \cite{CMS-Main,CMS-Muon,CMS-Phase2Muon}. The drift velocity for electrons with this configuration is approximately \cite{DT-CosmicsCalibrationVelocity}:
\begin{equation}
    v_\text{drift}\approx\SI{54.5}{\frac{\micro\meter}{\nano\second}}.
    \label{eq:DT-DriftVelocity}
\end{equation}
With the half-width of $d/2\approx\SI{21}{\mm}$ of a cell, the maximum electron drift time is:
\begin{equation}
    t_\text{drift,max}=\frac{d/2}{v_\text{drift}}\approx\SI{385}{\nano\second}.
    \label{eq:DT-MaxDriftTime}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Performance}
\label{sec:CMS-DTPerformance}
%\begin{itemize}
%    %\item Relevance of muon detection (e.g. dimuon Higgs decay)
%    \item DT performance (efficiency and active channels)
%    \item DT system resolution
%\end{itemize}
The \ac{DT} system provides essential muon detection and reconstruction capabilities in the \ac{CMS} barrel region. This section should briefly present the performance of the subdetector.
\newline The \ac{DT} chambers in \ac{CMS} have proven excellent reliability. The global fraction of active channels in 2024 was given with $98.65\%$. This value includes known problems in few chambers of the detector, which will be fixed in planned interventions \cite{DT-Performance2024}.
\newline For the Phase-2 upgrade (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-Overview}), extensive longevity studies were performed to analyze the expected performance of the \ac{DT} system under the tightened radiation environment. The studies show ageing effects of the chambers, however acceptable performance is expected for the Phase-2 operations. Therefore, the chambers themselves will not be exchanged during the upgrade \cite{CMS-Phase2Muon}.
\newline The efficiency of a muon to trigger a cell on its track in 2024 was calculated to be $96.93\%$ \cite{DT-Performance2024}. The spatial resolution of one cell lies in the order of \SI{250}{\micro\meter}, giving the full chamber a resolution of \SI{100}{\micro\meter} in the $r$-$\phi$-direction \cite{CMS-Main}.

\subsection{Frontend electronics}
\label{sec:CMS-DTFrontend}
The frontend electronics of the \ac{DT} system are located inside the chamber gas volume. They amplify the analog pulse from the drift cell wire and compare it to an amplitude threshold, providing a digital pulse on the output \cite{CMS-Main,CMS-Muon}. Because of their sufficient radiation hardness and their limited accessibility, these are the only part of the electronics to not be exchanged in the course of the Phase-2 upgrade \cite{CMS-Phase2Muon}.
\paragraph{MAD chip}
A custom \ac{ASIC} called \enquote{MAD} \cite{DT-MADChip} was developed for the frontend electronics, which houses the analog amplification and signal shaping circuits. One chip has inputs for four \ac{DT} wire signals. The charge to voltage conversion gain of the mounted chip is around \SI{3.4}{\milli\volt/\femto\coulomb}. The MAD chip also includes a discriminator stage (sec.\ \ref{sec:ReadoutElectronics-General}), which converts the analog signal into a digital pulse. The threshold of the discriminator can be set externally and usually is in the range $\mathcal{O}(\SI{30}{\milli\volt})$. Note that the digital output of the MAD chip is not single-ended, but in the form of a \ac{LVDS} pulse \cite{CMS-Main,CMS-Muon}. Fig.\ \ref{fig:MAD-DieShot} shows a die shot of the MAD chip.
\paragraph{Frontend board}
The \ac{FEB} is the \ac{PCB} which hosts the MAD chips. It connects the \ac{DT} wire pins with the MAD inputs and the \ac{LVDS} outputs of the MAD to the \acp{FEC} located on the casing of the \ac{DT} chamber. The \ac{FEB} is also wired to so-called testpulse connectors on the chamber casing. These are coupled to the inputs of the MAD to emulate a wire hit, when a testpulse signal is received. This testpulse capability is required for precise timing calibration between all channels (sec.\ \ref{sec:DTData-Testpulse}). A photo of one \ac{FEB} is shown in fig.\ \ref{fig:DT-Frontend}.

\subsection{Minicrate electronics}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Minicrate location on chamber
%    \item Minicrate functionalities
%    \item On-Board Electronics for Drift Tubes boards
%\end{itemize}
\label{sec:CMS-DTMinicrate}
The so-called minicrate electronics are the digital readout electronics of the \ac{DT} system. They are mounted directly on the outside frame of the chamber. The minicrates perform the data acquisition and timestamp assignment of the \ac{LVDS} hits coming from the \acp{FEB} in the chamber. While the legacy minicrates already performed hit selection for the \ac{L1T}, the Phase-2 electronics eliminate this additional complexity, as all trigger tasks are handled by the backend system. Apart from hit data acquisition, the minicrates also have slow control capabilities to interface \ac{DT} and \ac{RPC} infrastructure, including \ac{FEB} channel masking, gas pressure monitoring and control of the muon alignment system hardware \cite{CMS-Main,CMS-Muon,CMS-Phase2Muon}.
\paragraph{On-Board Electronics for Drift Tubes}
The \ac{OBDT} boards are the heart of the Phase-2 minicrates. They receive the \ac{LVDS} pulses from the chamber and assign timestamps to all hits \cite{CMS-Phase2Muon,CMS-Phase2}. There exist two flavors of boards, mainly due to the different \ac{FEC} types used on the differently oriented \acp{SL} \cite{DT-Phase2Upgrade}: The \ac{OBDT} phi \cite{DT-Phase2Upgrade}, developed at \ac{INFN} Padova, Italy and the \ac{OBDT} theta \cite{OBDT-theta-1,OBDT-theta-2}, developed at \ac{CIEMAT} Madrid, Spain. The \ac{OBDT} theta includes an auxiliary testpulse generation board, which was developed at Physics Institute III A of RWTH Aachen University. Both \ac{OBDT} flavors host a raditation-tolerant Microsemi PolarFire\textsuperscript{\textregistered} \ac{FPGA} \cite{PolarFire-FPGA}. Figs.\ \ref{fig:OBDT-phi} and \ref{fig:OBDT-theta} show photos of both \ac{OBDT} board flavors.

\newline The \acp{OBDT} of all minicrates run synchronously with a clock received from the backend system via an optical link. Slow control command and clock reception is handled by the \ac{lpGBT} \ac{ASIC} \cite{lpGBT-Main} (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-lpGBTMezzanine-lpGBT}) which is part of both \ac{OBDT} types. The clock reception logic ensures that all \acp{OBDT} actually run on the same clock and no time drift develops between different boards. Note that the \acs{OBDT} have a secondary slow control interface which does not rely on the \ac{lpGBT} \ac{ASIC} itself, but rather on duplicated logic implemented directly on the \ac{FPGA}: This provides redundancy for the system. The \ac{OBDT} also hosts the \ac{SCA} \ac{ASIC} \cite{SCA-Main} which provides services to the chamber peripherals connected to the minicrate.
\newline The assignment of timestamps to the hits is achieved with a \ac{TDC} logic (sec.\ \ref{sec:ReadoutElectronics-General}) running on the \ac{FPGA} with a time granularity of around $\SI{0.78}{\nano\second}$.\footnote{This granularity arises from the fact that the \ac{TDC} has a resolution of five bit and operates synchronously with the \ac{LHC} bunch crossing frequency (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-LHC}): $25/2^5\;\si{\nano\second}=25/32\;\si{\nano\second}\approx\SI{0.78}{\nano\second}$.} Each \ac{OBDT} board can receive inputs of 240 channels. In contrast to the legacy minicrate system, in Phase-2 all hits from the \ac{DT} cells are transferred to the backend electronics located in the service cavern of \ac{CMS}. The time-tagged hits are streamed out via two (redundant) \SI{10.24}{\giga\bit/\second} optical links in the transmission protocol of the \ac{lpGBT} \cite{CMS-Phase2Muon,CMS-Phase2}.
\paragraph{Testpulse generation}
The \ac{DT} system uses testpulses for timing calibration (sec.\ \ref{sec:DTData-Testpulse}). The \acp{OBDT} are responsible for testpulse generation of the \acp{FEB} they are connected to. The testpulses are required to have deterministic behavior, i.e.\ be issued synchronously to all channels, with offsets below \SI{1}{\nano\second}.\footnote{Due to different testpulse generation circuitry in \ac{OBDT} phi and \ac{OBDT} theta, there exists a constant timing offset between issued testpulses of the two boards. Since this offset is constant and can be measured beforehand, it does not affect the calibration negatively (sec.\ \ref{sec:DTData-Testpulse}).} The testpulse generation circuit on the \ac{OBDT} phi is located on the \ac{PCB} itself. For the \ac{OBDT} theta, an auxiliary board developed in Aachen is used for this purpose, as mentioned above \cite{DT-Phase2Upgrade}.
\paragraph{Safety system}
The \ac{MONSA} system is the interface of the central \ac{CMS} detector safety system to the \ac{DT} minicrate electronics and is developed at \ac{INFN} Torino, Italy. The system can monitor and disable each \ac{OBDT} of all minicrates individually. The \ac{OBDT} boards feature a custom interface for the \ac{MONSA}, which connects to the \ac{MONSA} backend services via the existing copper cables in the muon barrel which were used for the legacy minicrates and are no longer needed for other purposes in the Phase-2 system \cite{DT-Phase2Upgrade}.
\paragraph{Minicrate assembly}
The \acp{OBDT} as well as all cables and periphery are housed in an aluminum frame, which resembles the actual minicrate. The minicrate is mounted on one side of the \ac{DT} chamber, in a gap between both phi-oriented \acp{SL}. The connections between the minicrate and the backend infrastructure that are required to operate it are the following: Low voltage supply, slow control optical fiber, hit data optical fibers, \ac{MONSA} cables \cite{DT-Phase2Upgrade,CMS-Phase2Muon}. Note that because of the different chamber sizes in different stations, there exist several types of minicrates, equipped with different numbers of \acp{OBDT} and therefore readout channels. Fig.\ \ref{fig:Minicrate-MB1} shows a picture of an assembled minicrate for an MB1 chamber.

\paragraph{Chamber construction in Aachen}
The development and construction of gaseous detectors has a long history at Physics Institute III A of RWTH Aachen University. The institute was already active in one of the first larger collaborations in experimental particle physics at \acs{CERN}, the UA-1 collaboration \cite{UA1-Main}. In the \ac{CMS} collaboration \cite{CMS-Main}, the institute took over the responsibility of constructing about one quarter of the required \ac{DT} chambers \cite{RWTH-History}.

\subsection{Coordinate frame and timestamp format}
\label{sec:CoordinateFrame-TimestampFormat}
As a hit reconstruction is performed, establishing a coordinate frame convention in this work is required. It should be briefly introduced here, together with the format of the hit timestamps recorded with the Phase-2 electronics.
\paragraph{Coordinate frame}
The \ac{DT} chamber is oriented horizontally in the test stand.  A local coordinate system is defined for the analysis of the \ac{DT} data: The $x$-axis is chosen parallel to the theta superlayer wires, the $y$-axis parallel to the superlayer phi wires. The $z$-axis is chosen to point upwards. The origin of the coordinate frame is chosen as the lower corner of the \ac{DT} chamber, more precisely the bottom left corner of the lowest drift cell layer. Fig.\ \ref{fig:ThisCoordinateFrame} illustrates the coordinate frame used in this work.
\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{ThisCoordinateFrame.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of the coordinate frame convention in this work. The \ac{SL}, layer (Ly) and wire (Wi) numbering is also indicated \cite[adapted]{DT-LocalReco}.}
    \label{fig:ThisCoordinateFrame}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{Timestamp format}
The \acp{OBDT} naturally send out the hits with a timestamp consisting of two parts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TDC value:} It is the conversion result of the 5-bit \ac{TDC} running on the \ac{OBDT}. The conversion runs on the scope of \SI{25}{\nano\second}, with the mentioned time granularity of $\SI{0.78}{\nano\second}$ (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-DTMinicrate}).
    \item \textbf{BX value:} In the context of the \ac{CMS} experiment, the $BX$ value is the bunch crossing number of the collider. With the crossing frequency of \SI{40}{\mega\hertz} (sec.\ \ref{sec:CMS-LHC}), one $BX$ unit corresponds to the crossing period of \SI{25}{\nano\second}. The $BX$ value has a range of $0-3563$, as this corresponds to the \ac{LHC} accelerator filling scheme.
\end{itemize}
The backend firmware assigns an additional, higher level timestamp to the hits received from the \acp{OBDT}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{OC value:} The abbreviation $OC$ refers to orbit count and corresponds to the \ac{LHC} orbit identifier in the context of \ac{CMS}. It counts up one unit when the $BX$ value overflows (meaning $1OC=3564BX$). One $OC$ unit corresponds to \SI{89.1}{\micro\second}. The backend firmware allows for orbit counter values of up to $2^{26}$, before also the $OC$ value overflows.
\end{itemize}
From these three time values, a single integer timestamp value $T$ is constructed in the offline analysis code, which is used in the following to describe the timing of recorded hits:
\begin{equation}
    T(OC,\;BX,\;TDC) = TDC + 32\cdot BX +  32\cdot 3564\cdot OC
    \label{eq:Timestamp}
\end{equation}
One \ac{TU} corresponds to the same time as one $TDC$ unit, i.e. \SI{0.78}{\nano\second}.
