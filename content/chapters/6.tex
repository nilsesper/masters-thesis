\section{Discriminator threshold calibration}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item FPGA-internal comparators (on FPGA silicon die) are subject to production uncertainties
%    \item Unfolds in voltage bias on comparator switching point
%    \item Scan input voltage range while keeping other comparator input constant to detemine switching point of comparator (Metastability calibration)
%    \item Account for measured biases when setting thresolds
%\end{itemize}
\label{sec:ScintData-VoltageBias}
The \ac{FPGA}-internal comparators used for discrimination of the analog \ac{SiPM} signals have individual bias voltages (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard-FPGASampling}). An ideal comparator would switch its output state at the point where both input voltages are equal: $V_+ = V_-$. For the non-ideal comparator, the switching point is shifted by the bias voltage: $V_+ + V_\text{bias} = V_-$. As explained, the bias voltage is expected to be as high as $\pm 35 \;\si{\milli\volt}$ \cite{UltraScale-FPGA-SelectIO-Resources}. As the discriminator threshold lies in the range of $\mathcal{O}(\SI{10}{\milli\volt})$, a calibration of the bias voltage for each individual comparator is essential.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Measurement with function generator}
There are several ways to measure the real switching point of the comparator and extract the bias. The established method is to use a well-defined triangular pulse generated by a function generator for this purpose. By measuring the timestamps of the comparator switching points one can calculate the bias \cite{FPGA-MVT-Xi,FPGA-MVT-Eliseev}. The advantage of this method is that one measures the rising and falling edges of the comparator separately, i.e.\ one can conclude whether the comparator shows a hysteresis effect. However, the conducted measurements indicate no significant hysteresis.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Measurement with comparator metastability}
A novel approach for the measurement of the comparator bias was introduced by our group \cite{MSc-Ehlert}, which eliminates the need for an external function generator. Instead, the method only requires an adjustable voltage source (an \ac{ADC}) at the signal input of the comparator. This significantly reduces the complexity of the calibration setup. In the case of the developed readout board (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard}), this functionality is already included on the \ac{PCB} itself.
\newline One input of the comparator is set to the desired discriminator threshold voltage: $V_-=V_\text{thres}$. The voltage of the other input is varied in the range $V_+ \in [V_\text{thres}-\SI{50}{\milli\volt},\; V_\text{thres}+\SI{50}{\milli\volt} ]$ with a fine step size. For each step, the count rate of the comparator switching events is measured. This is enabled by counting registers inside the \ac{FPGA} logic which can be read out and reset to zero after changing the voltage level. At the switching point of the comparator, the output is undefined. For an ideal device, one would expect a delta peak. For the real comparator, one would expect a peak in the count rate with a finite width, as electronic noise leads to small fluctuations in the comparator input voltages. By estimating the position of the peak, the bias voltage $V_\text{bias}$ can be estimated. Fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-VoltageBias-Scheme} illustrated the described calibration method exploiting the comparator metastability.
\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Scint-VoltageBias-Scheme.pdf}
    \caption{Illustration of the described metastability calibration method to measure the comparator bias voltage \cite[adapted]{MSc-Ehlert}.}
    \label{fig:ScintData-VoltageBias-Scheme}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Observed bias voltages}
The voltage scans are performed for all input channels of the \ac{FPGA} and the switching rate histogram is analyzed. Fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-VoltageBias-SwitchingCount} shows an example histogram of the switching rate depending on the applied \ac{DAC} voltage. The step size is limited by the accuracy of the used \ac{DAC} to \SI{2.5}{\milli\volt}. The count rate exhibits the expected shape with a clear peak. It should be noted that the peak position is not equal to the threshold voltage applied to the other input of the comparator, indicating a non-zero bias voltage.
\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering % presented histogram: ca3 zynq51 input4 step0
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{scint_thres_calib/single_count_hist.pdf}
    \caption{Example switching rate histogram for a single comparator of the \ac{FPGA}. The weighted mean of the distribution is marked in red. Initially it deviates from the threshold voltage (gray) applied to the other input of the comparator, because of the nonzero bias voltage.}
    \label{fig:ScintData-VoltageBias-SwitchingCount}
\end{figure}
\newline The bias voltage is calculated as difference between the switching rate peak position $\left\langle V_\text{DAC} \right\rangle$ and the expected peak position $V_\text{thres}$:
\begin{equation}
    V_\text{bias} = \left\langle V_\text{DAC} \right\rangle - V_\text{thres}.
    \label{eq:Scint-VoltageBias-BiasCalculation}
\end{equation}
 The result for the measured bias voltage for all 32 inputs of a readout board is presented below in fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-VoltageBias-CalibResult} with the blue data points. The obtained bias voltages lie in the range \SI{-10}{\milli\volt} to \SI{15}{\milli\volt}, which is compatible with the mentioned maximum range of $\pm 35 \;\si{\milli\volt}$ from the \ac{FPGA} vendor datasheet \cite{UltraScale-FPGA-SelectIO-Resources}. 
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Bias voltage correction}
After the measurement, the comparator threshold voltages are adjusted accordingly. To account for the obtained bias voltage, the new threshold voltage is selected as
\begin{equation}
    V_\text{thres,new} = V_\text{thres,initial} - V_\text{bias}.
    \label{fig:ScintData-VoltageBias-NewThresold}
\end{equation}
The procedure is repeated multiple times, in order to iteratively improve the precision of the calibration.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Validation measurement}
After setting the final threshold voltage, the bias voltage is measured one more time to validate the outcome of the calibration. The obtained validation data points are also shown in fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-VoltageBias-CalibResult}, with the read data points. As expected, the bias voltage could be mostly removed with this procedure, while few channels show a small remaining offset of $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\milli\volt})$. Repeating the validation measurements after a power-cycle or a longer time does not show significant changes, hinting that the bias voltage is stable in time. However (as described in sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard-FPGASampling}), the measured bias voltages depend on the applied threshold, making it necessary to repeat the calibration after the threshold is changed.

\section{TDC timing calibration}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Bank-specific calibration of deserializers on each power-cycle leads to non-deterministic signal delays
%    \item Use simultaneous testpulses to measure these delays
%    \item Use delay module in firmware to correct for these effects already on the device
%    \item Mention ringing of testpulse signal in circuit, but explain that no negative effect on calibration
%\end{itemize}
\label{sec:ScintData-TimingBias}
As explained in sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-SamplingTDC}, the \ac{TDC} logic of the readout board is realized in the \ac{FPGA} firmware by utilizing the deserializer primitives of the chip. On startup of the \ac{FPGA}, the deserializers are internally calibrated, individually for each component. This individual calibration leads to non-deterministic time offsets between different groups of inputs. 
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Simultaneous testpulse generation}
The readout board offers the possibility to transmit simultaneous testpulses to all input channels (sec.\ \ref{fig:Scint-ReadoutBoard-AnalogCircuit}). Similar to the \ac{DT} timing calibration with testpulses (sec.\ \ref{sec:DTData-Testpulse}), it is possible to extract timing offsets between the input channels by measuring the timestamps of the testpulse hits.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Testpulse response} 
The testpulses are issued at a fixed time relative to the beginning of an orbit (which starts at $BX=0$, $TDC=0$). The timestamps of the testpulse-induced hits in all channels is recorded. Once again, one can define the $T_\text{orbit}$ variable, which is the time of testpulse arrival after the beginning of the orbit (cf.\ sec.\ \ref{sec:DTData-Testpulse-Generation}). A histogram of the recorded testpulse hit timestamps for one channel is presented in fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-TimingBias-TPTimestamp}. The testpulse timing seems to be very sharp $\mathcal{O}(\SI{1}{\nano\second})$, with only two TDC bins showing entries in the histogram.
\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering % presented histogram: 
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{scint_timing_calib/single_timing_hist.pdf}
    \caption{Example histogram of testpulse arrival timing for one input channel of the readout board.}
    \label{fig:ScintData-TimingBias-TPTimestamp}
\end{figure}
\newline To quantify the testpulse timing of each channel with one value, the weighted mean $\left\langle T_\text{orbit} \right\rangle$ is calculated and used. The initially recorded testpulse timing for all 64 channels of the two used readout boards is presented in fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-TimingBias-CalibResult} with the blue data points. It is evident that there are several groups of inputs which lie closely together in time for each readout board. This behavior is expected, as the \ac{FPGA}-internal calibration of the deserializer units is happening on the scope of so-called I/O banks of the \ac{FPGA}. When mapping the input channels back to these I/O banks of the chip, it becomes evident that the two groups of input channels for each board in the timing diagram correspond to these banks (indicated with different data markers in fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-TimingBias-CalibResult}). With each power-cycle of the board, the calibration takes place again, meaning that the timing calibration is required after each startup of the board. The observed timing fluctuation is usually $\lesssim \SI{80}{\tu}$ (\SI{62}{\nano\second}).
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Timing correction with delay unit}
In principle, it would be possible to only extract the timing corrections from the measurement and do the actual correction of the recorded data offline. As the \ac{FPGA} firmware features a programmable delay unit for each input channel (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutFirmware-ProgrammableDelays}), it is even possible to program the delays according to the observed timing differences and perform this correction already "online". The method of determining the delays to be programmed for all input channels is discussed in this section.
\newline Because it is only possible to delay the timing of the input channels, the target timing for all channels has to be selected as the maximum observed timestamp of all channels:
\begin{equation}
    T_\text{orbit,target} = \max \left\{ T_{\text{orbit},i} \; ; \; i=0,...,N_\text{chs} \right\} .
    \label{eq:ScintData-TimingBias-TimingTarget}
\end{equation}
In order to reach the desired timing of each input channel, one needs to subtract the observed testpulse timestamp $\left\langle T_\text{orbit} \right\rangle$ of the respective channel from the target value. For technical reasons, the delay unit in the firmware only allows to set the delay with a granularity of $T_\text{delaystep}=\SI{8}{\tu}$. This of course implies that the timing correction can not be better than this granularity. The delay register value to be programmed for channel $i$ is obtained by division and integer rounding of the target delay:
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{Delay\_Register}(i) = \text{round}\left( \frac{T_\text{orbit,target} - \left\langle T_{\text{orbit},i} \right\rangle}{T_\text{delaystep}} \right) .
    \label{eq:ScintData-TimingBias-DelayRegisterValue}
\end{equation}
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Validation measurement}
After setting the proper delay unit values, a validation measurement is performed with the testpulses. The resulting testpulse timing after the "online" timing correction is also presented in fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-TimingBias-CalibResult} in red. It is clearly visible that the testpulse response is now aligned within a window of $\approx\SI{8}{\tu}$ which corresponds to the expected accuracy limit of the correction due to the delay unit granularity. Note that the correction is simultaneously applied for the two readout boards, leading to the desired situation where all 64 channels are aligned in time, not only those channels which are connected to the same readout board.

\section{SiPM noise scan}
%\begin{itemize}
%    \item Scan of SiPM rates for different thresolds with developed readout system
%    \item Show resulting curve
%    \item Discuss visible exponential (expected) behavior
%    \item Aliasing effects (too coarse scanning steps) hide expected steps in spectrum
%\end{itemize}
\label{sec:ScintData-Noise}
Before atmospheric muon data is recorded with the system, the setup should be exercised by measuring the \ac{SiPM} noise rates for different discriminator threshold voltages. This is done as a plausibility check of the developed readout system as well as to check that the \acp{SiPM} on the detector are behaving as expected.
\newline Note that this measurement was only performed with one readout board and 32 \acp{SiPM}, and not with the full scintillation detector.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Discriminator threshold range}
In order to measure the \ac{SiPM} rates for different threshold voltage levels, it is first necessary to perform a discriminator threshold calibration (sec.\ \ref{sec:ScintData-VoltageBias}) for each voltage. This is necessary because the comparator bias measured with the calibration procedure is dependent on the selected threshold, as discussed above. After all calibration runs have been completed, the \acp{SiPM} of the scintillator can be connected to the readout board.
\newline The range of discriminator thresholds to be considered for the \ac{SiPM} noise measurement is $\SI{2.5}{\milli\volt}-\SI{32.5}{\milli\volt}$.  The \acp{DAC} which provide the threshold voltage $V_\text{thres}$ have a granularity of \SI{2.5}{\milli\volt}. The common voltage $V_\text{com}$ on the other side of the discriminator circuit is provided with a common \ac{DAC} with the same granularity (sec.\ \ref{sec:Scint-ReadoutBoard-InputCircuitry}).
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Rate measurement}
As one expects very high rates for low threshold values and the transmission bandwidth of hits to the backend is limited, the count rate of the inputs is measured directly on the readout board itself, with the counter registers that are also used for the comparator bias calibration (sec.\ \ref{sec:ScintData-VoltageBias}). From the measurement duration and the obtained hit count, it is trivial to calculate the rate present at the input. In this case, the number of hits is recorded for $\SI{10}{\second}$ per \ac{SiPM} and threshold voltage, which already gives a sufficiently high count to have reasonable uncertainties.
\newline Using the counter registers in the \ac{FPGA} firmware for the measurement has the described advantage of not being limited by the data transfer rate to the backend. On the other hand however, the counter register is directly attached to the input block of the firmware. This means that the dead time logic is not yet applied to the counted hits.
\newline As will be discussed in sec.\ \ref{sec:ScintData-Hits-Systematics} in detail, signal reflections and crosstalk are observed in measurements with the readout board. It is clear that these effects have an influence on the measured \ac{SiPM} rate. Since the measurement of the \ac{SiPM} noise spectrum in this section is only envisioned as a plausibility check, and not as a characterization measurement of the \ac{SiPM} device, this is accepted here.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Resulting SiPM rates}
Fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-Noise-ScanResult} presents the resulting rates of 29 \acp{SiPM} channels. Three channels had to be excluded from the measurement, because they showed unphysical behavior, which might be related to a defect in the \ac{SiPM} or its preamplifier, or in a defect comparator inside the \ac{FPGA}.
\newline Note that the estimated uncertainties on the rate from the Poisson counting error are negligible, and not even visible in the plot. However, the uncertainty of threshold voltage (on the $x$-axis) is significant, as the granularity of the selectable threshold is relatively large.\footnote{As both the $V_\text{thres}$ and $V_\text{com}$ inputs of the comparator are connected to \acp{DAC} with a granularity of $\Delta V_\text{DAC}=\SI{2.5}{\milli\volt}$, the resulting uncertainty of the effective discrimination thresold $V_\text{thres,eff}=V_\text{thres}-V_\text{com}$ is calculated to be $\sigma_\text{thres,eff}=\sqrt{2\cdot \left( \Delta V_\text{DAC}/\sqrt{12} \right)^2 }\approx \SI{1}{\milli\volt}$. This is almost as large as the measured threshold voltage steps.}
\begin{figure}[htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{scint_noise_scan/sipm_rates.pdf}
    \caption{Obtained rates of 29 \acp{SiPM} for different discrimination thresholds in the range $\SI{2.5}{\milli\volt}-\SI{32.5}{\milli\volt}$ presented in a logarithmic plot, measured with one readout board.}
    \label{fig:ScintData-Noise-ScanResult}
\end{figure}
\newline The measured \ac{SiPM} rates show a clearly visible exponentially falling behavior with increasing threshold voltages. Over the measured range with a width of \SI{30}{\milli\volt}, the rate decreases by around four orders of magnitude.
\newline Another observation is the strong fluctuations in rate between individual \acp{SiPM}. There are several cases where different \acp{SiPM} show rate differences of more than one order of magnitude for the same threshold voltage.
\FloatBarrier
\paragraph{Comparison to expectation}
As discussed in sec.\ \ref{sec:Scintillators-SiPMs}, the noise of \acp{SiPM} originates from thermal excitations of electrons in the sensitive region of the device. This noise results in a signal with one \ac{p.e.} amplitude. Due to the non-zero probability $p_\text{cross}>0$ for crosstalk between \ac{GAPD} cells on the \ac{SiPM}, the amplitude of the noise hit can be increased. For $N$ triggered cells, the amplitude increases to $N$ \ac{p.e.}, however the probability for this is suppressed by a factor $\propto \left( p_\text{cross} \right)^N$. Therefore, one expects an exponentially falling distribution of the noise rate with increasing discriminator threshold. The expected exponential behavior can be well observed in the recorded noise scan in fig.\ \ref{fig:ScintData-Noise-ScanResult}.
\newline The exponential decrease in count rate is expected to happen in discrete steps, as each step translates to the activation of another discrete \ac{GAPD} cell. Since the used frontend board of the \ac{SiPM} has a preamplifier with a conversion factor of $\approx \SI{5}{\milli\volt}$/\ac{p.e.}, which is in the same order of magnitude as the scan interval of \SI{2.5}{\milli\volt}, strong aliasing effects should be present. It is therefore not unexpected that one can not make out the discrete steps in the presented measurement.
